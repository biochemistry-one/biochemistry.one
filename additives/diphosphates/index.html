Food additives

Diphosphates (E450)

In brief

Diphosphates are phosphate salts that make dough rise and keep acidity stable, widely used in biscuits and cakes. In the gut they are split into ordinary phosphate, which is mostly absorbed and excreted in urine. European regulators set one daily limit for phosphorus from additives and natural food together.

What it is

Diphosphates are phosphate salts that make dough rise and keep acidity stable, widely used in biscuits and cakes 12.

E 450 comprises disodium (i), trisodium (ii), tetrasodium (iii), tetrapotassium (v), dicalcium (vi), calcium dihydrogen (vii) and magnesium dihydrogen (ix) salts of diphosphoric acid, also called pyrophosphates; INS numbers are identical 1.

How often it is used

In April 2019, 6,529 of 126,556 French packaged foods listed diphosphates, eighth among additives 2.

Of 106,489 NutriNet-Santé adults, 64.36% consumed them, mean 178.33 mg/day; 0.02% exceeded the acceptable daily intake 3.

In the body

In the gut, diphosphates become ordinary phosphate, mostly absorbed, then excreted in urine 1.

In rats, dietary tetrasodium diphosphate was almost completely hydrolysed; in humans, 80–90% of additive phosphorus is absorbed as orthophosphate 1. Proximal tubules reabsorb 80% or more of filtered phosphorus, regulated by parathyroid hormone and FGF23 1.

What studies show

In short human studies, extra phosphate raised a phosphate-regulating hormone and briefly impaired artery widening 45.

In 10 adults, a week of additive-rich food (606 mg/day more phosphorus, 58 mmol/day more sodium) raised FGF23 by 23% against a low-additive week 4. In 11 men, flow-mediated dilation 2 h after 1200 mg phosphorus was 5.02% against 8.42% after 400 mg 5. In 279 hyperphosphataemic haemodialysis patients, cluster-randomised advice to avoid phosphate additives changed serum phosphorus by −0.6 mg/dL (95% CI −1.0 to −0.1) against usual care over 3 months 6.

In NutriNet-Santé, diphosphate intake was not associated with cardiovascular disease or type 2 diabetes 78. Over a mean 6.7 years, premenopausal breast cancer occurred in 113 of 19,382 people in the top intake third and 61 of 16,560 in the bottom (HR 1.45, 1.04 to 2.02; modelled risk at age 40, 2.5% against 1.7%) 9.

EFSA’s group acceptable daily intake for phosphates, 40 mg phosphorus/kg body weight/day, counts all dietary phosphorus and does not apply in moderate to severe renal impairment; estimated whole-diet intake exceeded it at the mean in infants, toddlers and children 1.

Consensus statements

KDIGO suggests limiting dietary phosphate for hyperphosphataemia in chronic kidney disease G3a–G5D (2D), considering its source, additives included (not graded) 10.

Unresolved questions

After false-discovery-rate correction, the breast cancer trend and links of trisodium phosphate to coronary disease and tripotassium phosphate to diabetes lost significance 978.

    Finding
    E 450 comprises disodium diphosphate (E 450(i)), trisodium diphosphate (E 450(ii)), tetrasodium diphosphate (E 450(iii)), tetrapotassium diphosphate (E 450(v)), dicalcium diphosphate (E 450(vi)), calcium dihydrogen diphosphate (E 450(vii)) and magnesium dihydrogen diphosphate (E 450(ix)); JECFA specifications carry the same INS sub-numbers. Formulae are Na2H2P2O7, Na3HP2O7, Na4P2O7, K4P2O7, Ca2P2O7, CaH2P2O7 and MgH2P2O7; synonyms include disodium pyrophosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, tetrapotassium salt of diphosphoric acid, calcium pyrophosphate and acid calcium and acid magnesium pyrophosphate. Diphosphate, also named pyrophosphate, is M4P2O7; sodium diphosphates are made by neutralising phosphoric acid with sodium hydroxide and drying above 200 °C, which condenses phosphate groups. JECFA functional classes (Appendix A) include raising agent for E 450(i), (ii), (vi), (vii) and (ix), sequestrant for (i) and (iii), emulsifier for (ii), (iii), (v) and (vii), emulsion stabiliser for (ii), (vii) and (ix) and buffer, including acidity regulator, for (iii) and (vi). Phosphate additives are assumed to dissociate in the gastrointestinal tract; inorganic phosphorus from food additives is absorbed at about 80 to 90% as free orthophosphate, without metabolism, by passive diffusion and sodium-dependent active transport; NaPi-IIb (SLC34A2) predominates in the intestine and is modulated by active vitamin D and low phosphorus intake. About 200 mmol of phosphorus is filtered daily, 80% or more is reabsorbed in proximal tubules mainly via NaPi-IIa, and PTH and FGF-23 with Klotho influence reabsorption; excretion is via the kidney. In rats fed 5% tetrasodium diphosphate, diphosphate was undetected in faeces and urine, hydrolysis to orthophosphate in the gut was almost complete and about 85% was absorbed; toxicokinetic data on dicalcium and calcium dihydrogen diphosphate are lacking. The only significant adverse effect of phosphates in animal studies was kidney calcification and tubular nephropathy; a 90-day rat study of tetrasodium diphosphate gave a NOAEL of 500 mg/kg bw per day (116 mg P/kg bw per day). A group ADI of 40 mg/kg bw per day, expressed as phosphorus, was derived for E 338–341, E 343 and E 450–452 from a chronic rat NOAEL of 76 mg P/kg bw per day plus 91 mg P/kg bw per day background diet (167 mg P/kg bw per day) divided by an adjustment factor of 4; it covers phosphorus from all sources, natural ones included, and does not apply to people with moderate to severe reduction in renal function. Mean whole-diet exposure estimated from analytical data ranged across surveys from 16 mg P/kg bw per day in adults and the elderly to 85 mg P/kg bw per day in infants aged 12 weeks to 11 months; it exceeded the ADI at the mean for infants, toddlers and children, and at the 95th percentile also for adolescents (highest 95th percentile 123 mg P/kg bw per day, in toddlers). Exposure from food additives indicatively contributed 6 to 21% of total mean intake in all groups except infants and 12 to 30% in infants aged 12 weeks to 11 months. In the EU, phosphates (E 338–341, E 343, E 450–452) were authorised with maximum permitted levels of 500 to 20,000 mg/kg expressed as P2O5 in 104 uses and at quantum satis in four.

    Finding
    Recommendation 4.1.8: 'In patients with CKD G3a–G5D, we suggest limiting dietary phosphate intake in the treatment of hyperphosphatemia alone or in combination with other treatments (2D). It is reasonable to consider phosphate source (e.g., animal, vegetable, additives) in making dietary recommendations (Not Graded).'     Finding
