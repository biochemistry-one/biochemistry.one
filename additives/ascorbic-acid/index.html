Food additives

Ascorbic acid (E300)

In brief

Ascorbic acid is vitamin C, added to cakes, juices, soft drinks and many other foods to protect them from oxidation, and to bread flour. The gut absorbs small amounts almost completely, and the body breaks part of it down to oxalate, which with calcium forms most kidney stones. Regulators found no safety concern at the amounts added to food, while high-dose supplements were linked to more kidney stones in men.

What it is

Ascorbic acid is vitamin C, used in bakery wares, juices and drinks against oxidation and to treat flour 12.

EFSA assessed E300 (INS 300) with sodium ascorbate (E301) and calcium ascorbate (E302) 1.

How often it is used

Ascorbic acid is the fifth most frequent additive in French packaged foods: 7,919 of 126,556 products in 2019 3.

Of 106,489 French adults, 80.56% consumed it, a mean 15.85 mg/day 4.

In the body

The gut absorbs small doses almost fully, large doses less well; the kidneys excrete the excess 1.

Sodium-dependent transport absorbs up to 98% of low doses, 75% of 1 g and 16% of 12 g 1. Oxidised ascorbate is reduced back, or degraded irreversibly to mainly oxalate and L-threonate 1. After injected labelled ascorbate, 44% of urinary radioactivity was recovered as oxalate 5. At high doses, intestinal bacteria break down unabsorbed vitamin C 5.

What studies show

Vitamin C helps iron absorption, and high-dose supplements were linked to kidney stones in men 67.

Ascorbate keeps non-haem iron ferrous and raises its absorption 1.5- to 10-fold 6. In nitrite-cured meat it can lower nitrosamine levels except NDMA, NPYR, NPIP and NSAR 8.

Over a median 11.3 to 11.7 years, stone hazard ratios for total vitamin C of 1,000 mg/day or more, against under 90 mg/day, were 1.43 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.79) in men and 0.99 (0.90 to 1.09) in women 9. Over 11 years, Swedish men taking ascorbic acid supplements alone had 310 first stones per 100,000 person-years, non-users 163 (relative risk 1.92, 1.33 to 2.77) 7.

With copper or iron ions, ascorbic and benzoic acids can yield traces of benzene, a carcinogen 1. EFSA found no adverse effects in animals and judged a numerical acceptable daily intake unnecessary 1.

Unresolved questions

EFSA saw a strong indication that intake below 1,500 mg/day is not a stone risk factor, although in a cohort it cited the relative risk in men at 1,000 mg/day or more was 1.41 (1.11 to 1.80) 1. Crude counts in men were 260 stones in 81,316 person-years against 157 in 45,363; the excess was seen only after adjustment, chiefly for potassium 9. EFSA judged pro-oxidant effects with metal ions, seen in vitro, unconfirmed in vivo 1.

    Funding and finding
    Funding

    Finding
    E 300 (INS 300) is identical to L-ascorbic acid, C6H8O6, 176.12 g/mol. Absorption by sodium-dependent active transport is up to 98% at low doses, 80-90% up to 180 mg, 75% at 1 g and 16% at 12 g; above 80-100 mg/day most further absorbed ascorbate is excreted unchanged in urine. Dehydroascorbic acid can be reduced back to ascorbic acid or irreversibly hydrolysed to diketogulonic acid, oxidised mainly to oxalic and L-threonic acid. Sodium and calcium ascorbate dissociate fully in the stomach. In the presence of copper(II) or iron(III), small amounts of benzene can form from benzoic acid; German non-alcoholic beverages measured in 2010 contained less than the EU drinking-water limit of 1 ug/L. Pro-oxidant and mutagenic effects with redox-active metal ions in some in vitro systems have not been confirmed in vivo. No adverse effects in animal studies at the highest doses tested; bladder tumour promotion by 5% dietary sodium ascorbate in initiated male rats was attributed to sodium. Epidemiology indicated that vitamin C below 1500 mg/day is not a risk factor for kidney stones, although the cited cohort of Taylor et al. (2004) reported a relative risk of 1.41 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.80) in men at 1000 mg/day or more. JECFA allocated a group ADI 'not specified' in 1981; the Panel saw no need for a numerical ADI. In the EU, E 300-302 belong to Group I and are authorised at quantum satis in most foods, with 200 or 300 mg/kg in processed cereal-based foods and baby foods (FCS 13.1.3). In bread, industry reported its function as a flour treatment agent. Fine bakery wares, unprocessed fruit and vegetables, fruit juices and flavoured drinks were the main contributors to additive exposure.

  5. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA). Scientific Opinion on Dietary Reference Values for vitamin C EFSA Journal, 2013.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2013.3418

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (question EFSA-Q-2011-01229).
    Finding
    Vitamin C is an essential component of the diet, as humans and other primates have lost the ability to synthesise it. It is a cofactor of monooxygenases, dioxygenases and mixed function oxygenases, reducing the active-centre metal ion and maintaining it in a reduced state. Procollagen-proline dioxygenase and procollagen-lysine 5-dioxygenase require vitamin C for maximal activity, and deficiency impairs the synthesis of collagen, the principal protein of skin, bones, teeth and connective tissues. Scurvy occurs in adults at a plasma ascorbate below 10 umol/L and a body pool below 300 mg, with tooth loss, joint pain, poor wound healing, petechiae, bruising and inflamed, bleeding gums; an intake of 10 mg/day prevents it.

    Funding

    Finding
    Dietary iron is absorbed as Fe2+ and not as Fe3+; reducing agents, including vitamin C, promote non-haem iron absorption by keeping it reduced. Inorganic (non-haem) iron absorption is increased 1.5- to 10-fold depending on iron status, the dose of vitamin C and the test meal, although effects on haemoglobin when vitamin C is given with iron are modest. A cause and effect relationship was established between dietary intake of vitamin C and increased non-haem iron absorption.

    EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS), Mortensen A, Aguilar F, Crebelli R, Di Domenico A, Dusemund B, et al. Re-evaluation of potassium nitrite (E 249) and sodium nitrite (E 250) as food additives EFSA Journal, 2017.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4786PubMed 32625504

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority; requestor: European Commission (Question numbers EFSA-Q-2011-00460 and EFSA-Q-2011-00461).
    Finding
    Sodium and potassium nitrite are used in curing mixtures to develop and fix the colour of meat, to inhibit microbial growth and to develop characteristic flavours. Acceptable daily intake 0.07 mg nitrite ion/kg body weight per day, based on methaemoglobin formation. Use as food additives supplied about 17% (range 1.5 to 36.0%) of overall nitrite exposure. Margin of exposure for endogenous NDMA formed from nitrite at the ADI 4.2 × 10^5; for preformed NDMA plus NDEA in processed food, below 10,000 in all age groups at high exposure. Nitrosamines formed from nitrite added at authorised levels could not be clearly distinguished from those present without added nitrite. There was evidence linking preformed NDMA with colorectal cancer and some evidence linking nitrite plus nitrate from processed meat with colorectal cancer.
