Food additives

Mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471)

In brief

Mono- and diglycerides are fat molecules with one or two fatty acids on glycerol instead of three, used to keep oil and water mixed in bread, desserts, ice cream and margarine. They are digested like ordinary fat, and since 2023 the EU has limited glycidyl esters, contaminants that form when fats are strongly heated. In a large French cohort, people who ate more of them were diagnosed with cancer slightly more often, and whether the additive causes this is unknown.

What it is

Mono- and diglycerides are glycerol bound to one or two fatty acids; ordinary fat has three 12.

E471 (INS 471), an emulsifier, is a mixture of glycerol mono-, di- and triesters of fatty acids, usually made by glycerolysis of fats and oils, which may be hydrogenated 341. It is used mainly in desserts, ice cream, bread and margarine 1.

How often it is used

E471 is the tenth most frequent additive in French packaged foods, listed in 6,060 of 126,556 products in 2019 5.

Of 106,489 adults, 78.07% consumed it, at a cohort mean of 160.8 mg/day 6.

In the body

Digestive enzymes very likely split E471 into glycerol and fatty acids 1.

Enterocytes re-esterify absorbed fatty acids to triacylglycerol, mostly by the monoacylglycerol pathway 2. EFSA’s mean adult intake estimates equal 0.8 to 3.5% of reference fat intake 1.

In one donor’s faecal cultures, several measures were altered irreversibly by glyceryl stearate and one or none by two other E471 products 7.

What studies show

In a French cohort, higher E471 intake was linked to slightly more cancer 8; see also emulsifiers.

Among 92,000 adults over a mean 6.7 years, hazard ratios, highest against lowest third of intake, were 1.15 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.27) overall, 1.24 (1.03 to 1.51) for breast and 1.46 (1.09 to 1.97) for prostate cancer; modelled absolute risk at age 60 was 12.4% against 11.2% 8. E471 was not associated with type 2 diabetes over a mean 6.8 years 9.

EFSA judged a numerical acceptable daily intake unnecessary; JECFA set one “not limited” in 1973 1.

Above 200 °C, diglycerides form glycidyl esters, hydrolysed in the gut to glycidol, a genotoxic carcinogen in rodents 110.

Industry-funded results

An emulsifier manufacturers’ association reported up to 111.25 mg/kg glycidyl esters, as glycidol, and up to 59.92% trans fatty acids where fats were partially hydrogenated 11.

Unresolved questions

Crude cancer counts were 790 of 30,667 and 796 of 30,604 in the lowest and highest thirds 8. Over a median 7.4 years, cardiovascular disease was associated with E471 plus E472 esters (1.07 per SD, 1.04 to 1.11) but not with E471 alone 12. Intakes were estimated as doses, from diets of 2009–2021, before the EU limits 83. Mechanisms are yet to be elucidated 8.

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority; requestor: European Commission.
    Finding
    Mono- and diglycerides are very likely hydrolysed by gastrointestinal lipases to glycerol and fatty acids. No need for a numerical ADI and no safety concern at reported uses. At mean exposure E 471 supplies 0.8 to 3.5% of the recommended daily fat intake of adults. Specific studies of E 471 and the gut microbiota were not available. Monoglycerides and diglycerides are esters of glycerol in which one or two hydroxyl groups are esterified with a long-chain fatty acid; the JECFA definition states that E 471 is usually manufactured by glycerolysis of edible fats and oils but may also be prepared by esterification of fatty acids with glycerol, and glycerolysis of natural or hydrogenated fats and oils runs at 200 to 250 °C under alkaline catalysis. In Mintel's GNPD, E 471 was labelled on 33,090 EU food and beverage products in 2012 to 2017, mainly chilled desserts, edible ices, bread and bread products, sandwiches, baking ingredients and mixes, pasta and margarines, and on up to about 75% of dairy ice cream and frozen yoghurt. E 471 is authorised in Group I and at quantum satis in 77 of 84 food categories. Rats absorbed 97.8 ± 0.4% of cottonseed-oil monoglycerides, and in a model digestive system 1,3-diolein was nearly completely hydrolysed to free fatty acids. Glycidyl esters form mainly from diglycerides when vegetable oils are heated above 200 °C and are hydrolysed in the gastrointestinal tract to free glycidol, classified as probably carcinogenic to humans (IARC 2A) and as genotoxic and carcinogenic by the EFSA CONTAM Panel. Chronic studies of diacylglycerol oil at up to 7,800 mg/kg bw per day in mice and 2,000 mg/kg bw per day in rats showed no carcinogenic potential. JECFA established an ADI 'not limited' in 1973. Mean adult exposure in the non-brand-loyal scenario was 10 to 26 mg/kg bw per day. The Panel recommended specification limits for toxic elements, glycerol impurities, residual solvents, trans fatty acids, glycidyl esters and erucic acid, and more data on 3-MCPD and glycidyl esters.

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    This work was funded by the National Institutes of Health Grant DK088210 and US Department of Agriculture Grant WIS01442.
    Finding
    Narrative review. Triacylglycerol forms the bulk of animal fats and plant oils and accounts for approximately 95% of dietary fat. Pancreatic lipase prefers sn-1 and sn-3 ester bonds and yields fatty acids and sn-2 monoacylglycerol. In enterocytes the monoacylglycerol pathway, with MGAT2 and DGAT1 in the endoplasmic reticulum, accounts for more than 75% of postprandial intestinal triacylglycerol synthesis; DGAT1 is the only DGAT detected in human intestine. Triacylglycerol forms the core of ApoB-containing chylomicrons, whose assembly requires MTP.

  10. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM). Risks for human health related to the presence of 3- and 2-monochloropropanediol (MCPD), and their fatty acid esters, and glycidyl fatty acid esters in food EFSA Journal, 2016.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2016.4426

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Question number EFSA-Q-2014-00535).
    Finding
    3- and 2-MCPD fatty acid esters are produced in vegetable oils on refining; glycidyl esters form from diacylglycerols when oils are heated above 200 °C, for example during deodorisation. Mean middle-bound concentrations (µg/kg) of 3-MCPD from esters: sunflower seed oil 521, maize oil 503, soya bean oil 394, rapeseed oil 232, palm oil/fat 2,912; of glycidol from esters: sunflower 269, maize 650, soya bean 171, rapeseed 166, palm 3,955. Glycidol increased tumour incidence in several tissues of rats and mice, likely via a genotoxic mode of action. Animal studies show extensive hydrolysis of esterified 3-MCPD and glycidol after oral administration; with no in vivo data for glycidyl esters, dose-response considerations assumed complete hydrolysis of the esters to free glycidol after ingestion. There is strong evidence from in vitro data and some evidence from in vivo studies that glycidol is genotoxic.

    Data from one interested business operator (EFEMA, documentation 1 to 3): free and bound 3-MCPD, as free 3-MCPD, from below 0.1 to 4.45 mg/kg and glycidyl esters, as glycidol, from below 0.1 to 111.25 mg/kg in around 80 batches of E 471, the highest in E 471 from unsaturated rapeseed oil with about 40% monoglycerides; trans fatty acids 0.01% to below 1% in 15 samples from fully hardened, 0.34% to 1.59% in 12 from non-hardened and 15.88% to 59.92% in 7 from partially hardened raw materials; the operator stated that the manufacture uses no solvents. At the lowest technologically achievable levels proposed by the operator (glycidyl esters 10 mg/kg, 3-MCPD 2.5 mg/kg, erucic acid 20 g/kg), margins of exposure for glycidol were 785 to 4,130 against 25,000 of low concern, and reference values were exceeded for 3-MCPD in infants below 16 weeks and for glycidyl esters and erucic acid in all population groups. For infants below 16 weeks, exposure from infant formulae at the maximum permitted level of 4,000 mg/kg was 800 mg/kg bw per day at mean and 1,040 at high consumption (418 and 543 at the mean reported use level of 2,089 mg/kg); daily exposure to mono- and diacylglycerols from breast milk and from formula containing E 471 was of the same order of magnitude, and the Panel concluded that there is no reason for a safety concern for use in food categories 13.1.1 and 13.1.5.1. The Panel recommended lower limits for lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic and new limits for 3-MCPD, glycidyl esters and erucic acid in the EU specifications.

    US Food and Drug Administration. Mono- and diglycerides, 21 CFR 184.1505: direct food substances affirmed as generally recognized as safe Code of Federal Regulations, title 21, section 184.1505, read in the electronic Code of Federal Regulations, title 21 amended to 14 October 2026, 2026.

    https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-184.1505

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    US Food and Drug Administration; the text is federal regulation codified by the Office of the Federal Register.
    Finding
    Mono- and diglycerides consist of a mixture of glyceryl mono- and diesters, and minor amounts of triesters, prepared from fats or oils or fat-forming acids derived from edible sources; the most prevalent fatty acids include lauric, linoleic, myristic, oleic, palmitic and stearic. They are manufactured by the reaction of glycerin with fatty acids or with triglycerides in the presence of an alkaline catalyst and purified to a mixture of glycerides, free fatty acids and free glycerin containing at least 90 percent-by-weight glycerides, meeting the Food Chemicals Codex, 3rd edition (1981), p. 201. In accordance with 184.1(b)(1) the ingredient is used in food with no limitation other than current good manufacturing practice; the GRAS affirmation rests on use as a dough strengthener, emulsifier and emulsifier salt, flavoring agent and adjuvant, formulation aid, lubricant and release agent, solvent and vehicle, stabilizer and thickener, surface-active agent, surface-finishing agent and texturizer at levels not exceeding current good manufacturing practice. Source: 54 FR 7403, 21 February 1989, as amended at 57 FR 10616, 27 March 1992.
