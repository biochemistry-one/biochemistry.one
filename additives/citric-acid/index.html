Food additives

Citric acid (E330)

In brief

Citric acid is an acid found in lemons, and it is added to many processed foods and drinks to make them tart and keep them stable. The body makes citrate in its own energy pathway and breaks eaten citric acid down to carbon dioxide and water. Frequent acidic drinks can erode teeth, and a link with type 2 diabetes comes from a mixture of additives, not from citric acid alone.

What it is

Citric acid occurs in many foods; lemon juice contains about 7% 1.

It is 2-hydroxy-1,2,3-propanetricarboxylic acid, INS 330, used as an acidulant, antioxidant synergist, sequestrant and flavouring agent 2. Commercial production includes fermentation with Aspergillus niger 34.

How often it is used

Citric acid is the most frequent additive in French packaged foods 5.

In April 2019 it was listed in 18,302 of 126,556 products 5. Between 2009 and 2020, 91.15% of 106,489 NutriNet-Santé adults consumed it, at a weighted mean of 1956.51 mg/day 6.

In the body

Citrate is made in the body, and eaten citric acid is broken down to carbon dioxide and water 13.

The sodium-coupled transporter SLC13A2 moves divalent citrate into intestinal and renal proximal tubule cells 7. Citrate is an intermediate of the mitochondrial citric acid cycle 8.

Unresolved questions

In a study of undetermined funding in six adults, serum and urinary citrate were raised by 40 mEq of oral citric acid without change in acid–base values, which the authors attribute to absorbed citrate 9.

What studies show

Teeth can be eroded by frequent or large intakes of citric acid 1.

In a meta-analysis of nine paediatric studies, dental erosion was associated with soft drinks (odds ratio 2.41, 95% CI 2.03 to 2.85) and vitamin C (1.16, 1.10 to 1.22); citric acid itself was not analysed 10.

In NutriNet-Santé, a soft-drink additive mixture led by citric acid was associated with type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio 1.13, 1.08 to 1.18, per standard deviation of score) 11. Over a mean 7.7 years, diabetes was diagnosed in 384 of 36,214 adults in the top third and 355 of 36,215 in the bottom third 11.

In 1973 JECFA set a group acceptable daily intake “not limited” for citric acid and its calcium, potassium, sodium and ammonium salts 2.

Unresolved questions

In ten men, in a fixed-order study, mean blood aluminium was 12 µg/L after an aluminium hydroxide antacid and 23 µg/L when lemon juice was added 12. In four case reports, fatigue and joint pain were linked to manufactured citric acid, with Aspergillus niger residues proposed as the cause 13. Both have undetermined funding 1213. In commercial batches assessed by EFSA, malformin C, aflatoxins and ochratoxin A were undetectable 3.

    Funding

    Finding
    Citric acid is an intermediary substance in oxidative metabolism, engaged in the tricarboxylic acid cycle; citric acid and citrates occur in many foods and are normal metabolites in the body. Ingestion of citric acid frequently or in large doses may cause erosion of teeth and local irritation, apparently because of the low pH; the effects also occur with lemon juice, which contains about 7% citric acid and has a pH below 3. In rats, diets with 1.2% citric acid had no harmful effect on growth or reproduction over two successive generations and 90 weeks, and dental attrition was slightly more marked than in controls; 1380 mg/kg bw per day given orally to three dogs for 112 to 120 days produced no symptoms or renal damage. Citric acid and its calcium, potassium and sodium salts do not constitute a significant toxicological hazard to man; acceptable daily intake not limited.

Scientific Opinion on the safety and efficacy of citric acid when used as a technological additive (acidity regulator) for all animal species EFSA Journal 13(2):4010; version published on 22 March 2016, replacing that of 17 February 2015, 2015.

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority; on request from the European Commission, question EFSA-Q-2013-00612, adopted on 27 January 2015. The application was submitted by FEFANA asbl (companies Impextraco, Provimi and Selko), and the opinion is based in part on data from a consortium of three companies producing or distributing citric acid.
    Finding
    Citric acid occurs in all living organisms as an intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle; it is a normal constituent of the diet of humans and animals and, when ingested, is rapidly and completely metabolised to carbon dioxide and water. pKa values 3.14, 4.77 and 6.39. Citric acid (E 330) is authorised as a food additive for general use following the quantum satis principle, with limits for some products such as juices and infant foods; JECFA (1974) allocated an ADI 'not limited' for citric acid and its calcium, potassium and sodium salts, a position retained by the Scientific Committee for Food in 1991. The three applicant companies produce citric acid by fermentation of carbohydrate-based substrates with non-genetically modified strains of Aspergillus niger, followed by removal of solids, purification, concentration and crystallisation; the dossier documented absence of viable cells of the production strain in the final product. Malformin C was below the limit of detection (1 µg/kg) in eight commercial batches from the three companies; aflatoxins B1, B2, G1 and G2 and ochratoxin A were below the limits of detection in 13 batches. Rumen microbes rapidly metabolise citric acid to carbon dioxide and acetic acid (Wright, 1971). The use of citric acid in animal nutrition is safe for the consumer.
