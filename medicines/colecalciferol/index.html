Medicines

Colecalciferol (vitamin D3)

In brief

Too much colecalciferol raises blood calcium, which can damage the kidneys and, when severe, disturb heart rhythm or cause coma and death; with calcium it increased kidney stones, very large yearly or monthly doses led to more falls in older people, and infants with certain inherited defects develop severe hypercalcaemia on preventive doses. Its active hormone also acts on the parathyroid glands, bone, the phosphate hormone FGF23 and immune cells, and in mice on the hormones that regulate blood pressure. In large trials of adults who mostly had enough vitamin D it did not prevent fractures, falls, cancer or heart disease; with calcium it prevented about 1 hip fracture per 1000 older people a year in the community and 9 per 1000 in institutions.

In intoxication, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, or 25(OH)D, accumulates and parathyroid hormone (PTH) is suppressed; bone resorption and intestinal absorption raise calcium 12. A UK label puts the adult intoxication threshold at 40,000 to 100,000 IU daily for 1 to 2 months; infants may react to far less 2. Calcium normalises over weeks to months after stopping; soft-tissue damage may be permanent 212.

Unresolved questions

Harms were under-measured: VITAL excluded people with previous hypercalcaemia or renal failure, had a placebo run-in and mailed side-effect questionnaires; D2d omitted 24-hour urine calcium; WHI stones were self-reported; 14 of 34 high-dose trials monitored urine calcium 131415512. Bolus results conflict: falls 52% against 53% with 100,000 IU monthly in ViDA (5108 adults, post hoc); 66.9% on 60,000 IU monthly against 47.9% on 24,000 IU in a partly industry-funded trial (200 adults) 1617.

Unresolved questions

Muscle VDR expression is contested in mouse studies 2425. Tibial artery calcification did not progress differently on 400 to 10,000 IU daily over 3 years (302 adults) 26. For calcium with or without vitamin D, 6 heart attacks or strokes caused and 3 fractures prevented per 1000 over 5 years were estimated; UK advisers judged the evidence unconvincing 2728.

What it is for and how it works

Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium from food 18.

Vitamin D3 400 IU daily prevents deficiency rickets in children, in which 25(OH)D is usually below 10 ng/mL 18. Liver CYP2R1 and kidney CYP27B1 hydroxylate colecalciferol to calcitriol, which binds VDR and increases intestinal TRPV6 and calbindin-D9k 2918.

In the body

Colecalciferol is stored mainly in fat 12.

The half-life of 25(OH)D is 13 to 15 days, of serum vitamin D3 about 24 hours 1230. Thiazides reduce urinary calcium excretion and raise hypercalcaemia risk; vitamin D3 may increase digitalis toxicity 21.

Genetic variation

Some infants inherit defects that make preventive vitamin D raise blood calcium severely 9.

Biallelic loss of CYP24A1, which degrades calcitriol, or of SLC34A1, a renal phosphate transporter, causes this; in Poland, cases rose with prophylaxis of 300,000 units three times a year, and all 11 historical cases tested carried mutations 9.

Benefit against harm

Across 81 trials, vitamin D did not reduce fractures or falls 31.

Of those tested in VITAL, 12.7% had 25(OH)D below 20 ng/mL; in ViDA, 24.9% 1322.

Consensus statements

USPSTF (2018) judged the stone harm small and recommends against 400 IU or less with 1000 mg calcium or less for primary fracture prevention after menopause 35.

Unresolved questions

Mortality conflicts: 11.0% against 11.4% in 38 vitamin D3 trials (RR 0.94, 0.91 to 0.98), but with more than 8% dropout, worst-case analyses allowed a large increase or decrease; 5.3% against 5.1% in D-Health (HR 1.04, 0.93 to 1.18) 3637.

Without the medicine

Exercise prevents falls in older people living in the community 38.

Falls were 0.77 times control (59 trials, 12,981 people), 195 fewer per 1000 a year; fall-related fractures RR 0.73 (low certainty) 38. Sunlight makes vitamin D3 in skin; UVB intoxication has not been reported 19.

How the recommendations were set

Europe’s upper limit derives from the lowest dose that repeatedly raised urine calcium 12.

EFSA divided 250 µg (10,000 IU) daily, with recurrent hypercalciuria in 11.3% against 4.0% on 10 µg, by 2.5, giving 100 µg daily 12.

Consensus statements

The IOM (2011) set 600 IU daily to age 70 and 800 IU from 71 39. The Endocrine Society (2011), whose chair declared interests including Merck, Amgen and Dairy Management Inc., defined deficiency below 20 ng/mL, which IOM members said inflates the number deemed deficient 4041. In 2024 the Society endorsed no sufficiency level and suggested against routine testing 42.

Unresolved questions

Among 156 articles cited by 47 bone-health guidelines, those with missing or unclear conflict declarations more often favoured vitamin D or calcium (RR 1.56, 1.05 to 2.31) 43.

Stopping

After stopping, blood levels fall over weeks to months 126.

Two years after VITAL ended, autoimmune disease no longer differed: 255 cases against 259 (HR 0.98) 14.

Unresolved questions

A PubMed search in October 2026 located no randomised withdrawal trial; evidence comes from post-trial follow-up and 25(OH)D kinetics 146.

A prescription is changed together with the prescriber.

  41. Expert consensus

    Rosen CJ, Abrams SA, Aloia JF, Brannon PM, Clinton SK, Durazo-Arvizu RA, et al. IOM committee members respond to Endocrine Society vitamin D guideline Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism, 2012.

    doi:10.1210/jc.2011-2218PubMed 22442278

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    The authors worked independently and voluntarily to develop the manuscript; no funding sources were used.
    Finding
    Members of the 2011 IOM committee disagree that serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D of 30 ng/ml or higher is needed, finding no bone-health benefit above about 20 ng/ml, and disagree that all persons below 20 ng/ml are deficient. They state that 16 ng/ml (40 nmol/liter) meets the needs of about half the population and that 20 ng/ml assures bone health in practically all persons (97.5%); using 20 ng/ml as a cutoff inflates the number of persons deemed deficient. They argue that the guideline incorrectly characterises several large subgroups as at risk and lacks evidence of benefit from high intakes.
