Metabolism

Fed and fasting

In brief

After a meal, insulin from the pancreas promotes the storage of glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscle and of fat in fat tissue. Between meals, insulin falls and glucagon rises; the liver releases glucose from glycogen and then makes most of it anew from lactate, amino acids and glycerol. In longer fasts the liver converts fat into ketone bodies, which become the brain's main fuel and so spare muscle protein.

Insulin and glucagon

After a meal the pancreas releases insulin, which promotes fuel storage; between meals insulin falls, glucagon rises and stored fuel is released 1.

Insulin signals through its tyrosine kinase receptor, insulin receptor substrates and AKT; in muscle, heart and adipose tissue this translocates GLUT4 glucose transporters to the plasma membrane 1. In hepatocytes, AKT phosphorylates FOXO1 and excludes it from the nucleus, which lowers transcription of PCK1 and G6PC1 2. Glucagon raises hepatic cAMP; protein kinase A then inactivates glycogen synthase, activates glycogen phosphorylase through phosphorylase kinase and lowers fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, an inhibitor of fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 2. Insulin also suppresses adipose lipolysis 21; see insulin and glucagon.

Glycogen

The liver stores glucose as glycogen after a meal and releases it between meals 3.

Glucose 6-phosphate activates liver glycogen synthase allosterically, and glucose favours the inactivation of glycogen phosphorylase 2. In eight healthy adults, liver glycogen fell overnight from 350 to 207 mmol/L and peaked at 316 mmol/L a mean 318 minutes after an 824-kcal liquid meal with 67.3% of its energy as glucose 3. After an overnight fast, ethanol lowered plasma glucose below 50 mg/100 ml in five of seven patients with previous alcohol hypoglycaemia, and glucagon did not raise it 4.

Gluconeogenesis

Between meals the liver, and in long fasts also the kidney, makes glucose from lactate, amino acids and glycerol 25.

Pyruvate carboxylase, activated by acetyl-CoA, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase bypass the irreversible steps of glycolysis 1.

β-Oxidation degrades even-chain fatty acids to acetyl-CoA 6, which contributes no net carbon to gluconeogenesis 2. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, and the citric acid cycle releases two CO2 for each acetyl-CoA, leaving no net oxaloacetate for glucose 1. The glyoxylate cycle, which bypasses these decarboxylations in plants and bacteria, has not been demonstrated in mammals 17.

Ketone bodies

When insulin is low and fat breakdown high, the liver converts fatty acids into ketone bodies, a fuel for the brain, heart and skeletal muscle 8.

Malonyl-CoA, formed in the fed state, inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, which mediates the entry of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria 98.

Hepatocytes lack succinyl-CoA:3-oxoacid CoA transferase, so the liver exports ketone bodies; other tissues activate acetoacetate with it, without ATP 8. Total ketone bodies rise from about 100–250 µM to about 1 mM after 24 hours of fasting and up to 20 mM in diabetic ketoacidosis 8.

The course of a fast

As a fast lengthens, the body shifts from glycogen to new glucose and then to fat and ketone bodies, sparing muscle protein 710.

Gluconeogenesis supplied 47% of glucose production after 14 hours of fasting, 67% after 22 hours and 93% after 42 hours 11. In six healthy men fasting for 180 hours, plasma glucose levelled off by the third day and 3-hydroxybutyrate rose from 0.016 to 4.23 mmol/L 7. Their urinary nitrogen peaked on the fourth day, then declined 7. In three obese adults fasting for 38 to 41 days, 3-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate had replaced glucose as the brain’s main fuel 10. In obese adults after 5 to 6 weeks, the kidney made 45% of the glucose produced, which totalled about 86 g a day 5.

Unresolved questions

Whether insulin suppresses hepatic glucose production mainly within the liver or through adipose lipolysis is disputed: mice lacking hepatic AKT1, AKT2 and FOXO1 suppressed it normally, yet a selective rise in portal insulin suppressed it in dogs and humans 2. How metformin lowers gluconeogenesis, through AMPK, complex I or mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, is unsettled 2. Acetone, formed from acetoacetate, may link fat to glucose: its carbon enters plasma glucose in fasting adults, but net synthesis in humans is unproven 12.

    Finding
    Educational review. Insulin, released after eating, binds the insulin receptor, a tyrosine kinase that autophosphorylates and recruits insulin receptor substrate, leading to phosphorylation of AKT; in skeletal muscle, heart and adipose tissue insulin signalling translocates GLUT4 from internal vesicles to the plasma membrane; insulin inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue; glucagon, released as blood glucose falls, acts mainly on the liver through a G-protein-coupled receptor that raises cAMP. Gluconeogenesis bypasses the irreversible steps of glycolysis with pyruvate carboxylase, activated by acetyl-CoA, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase. Because pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible and the carbon entering the citric acid cycle as acetyl-CoA is lost in its decarboxylation steps, fats cannot produce glucose at a net rate; the glyoxylate cycle, which bypasses these steps in plants, bacteria, fungi, algae and protozoa, is stated not to exist in animals.

    Finding
    Narrative review. Glucagon receptor (GCGR) activation raises cAMP and activates PKA, which inhibits L-type pyruvate kinase, phosphorylates PFK-2/FBPase-2 to favour its phosphatase activity and lower fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, an allosteric inhibitor of FBPase-1 (printed as fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 1), phosphorylates and inactivates glycogen synthase, and activates phosphorylase kinase, which phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase at Ser15; PKA also activates the CREB-CBP-CRTC2 complex for gluconeogenic gene transcription. FOXO proteins with PGC1-alpha raise PCK1 and G6PC expression and are excluded from the nucleus after AKT phosphorylation. Glucose enters hepatocytes by facilitated diffusion through GLUT2; glucose allosterically inhibits glycogen phosphorylase; glucose 6-phosphate is the key allosteric activator of GYS2. In overnight-fasted humans under somatostatin, hyperinsulinaemia was required to stimulate hepatic glycogen synthesis and hyperglycaemia to suppress glycogenolysis. Hepatic glucose production and storage are dysregulated in type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, and this imbalance contributes to hyperglycaemia in the fasted and postprandial states. Metformin is used as first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes and acts primarily through the suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis, by a molecular mechanism that remains under investigation. Several lines of evidence cast doubt on the necessity of AMPK: metformin continues to inhibit glucose production in Ampk-knockout primary mouse hepatocytes and improves glucose tolerance in Ampk-knockout mice to a similar extent as in wild-type mice. Inhibition of mitochondrial complex I is observed only at suprapharmacological, millimolar concentrations, in contrast to the clinically relevant plasma concentration range of 50 to 100 µM in patients who take 2 g of metformin daily. Metformin non-competitively inhibits mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase in isolated hepatocytes with a clinically relevant Ki of about 50 µM, which predicts impaired gluconeogenesis only from the redox-dependent substrates lactate and glycerol and not from pyruvate, alanine or dihydroxyacetone phosphate; the importance of the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle in gluconeogenesis in the human liver is unclear, so studies in humans are required.

    doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.12.022PubMed 28178565

    Funding

    Finding
    Review. Total ketone bodies in healthy adults oscillate between about 100 and 250 µM, rise to about 1 mM after 24 h of fasting or prolonged exercise and reach as high as 20 mM in diabetic ketoacidosis; the human liver produces up to 300 g per day. HMGCS2 catalyses the fate-committing condensation of acetoacetyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA; HMGCL releases acetyl-CoA and acetoacetate; BDH1 interconverts acetoacetate and D-3-hydroxybutyrate in a near-equilibrium NAD+/NADH-coupled reaction; acetoacetate decarboxylates spontaneously to acetone; MCT1 and MCT2 (SLC16A1, SLC16A7) release ketone bodies from cells. SCOT (OXCT1) is expressed in all mammalian cells with mitochondria except hepatocytes and activates acetoacetate without ATP; mitochondrial thiolases (ACAA2, ACAT1, HADHA or HADHB) yield two acetyl-CoA. Heart, skeletal muscle and brain oxidise ketone bodies, including during adherence to low-carbohydrate diets. Acetyl-CoA allosterically activates pyruvate carboxylase; malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT1. Hmgcs2 transcription is stimulated by FOXA2, which insulin inhibits through PI3K and Akt, induced by glucagon-cAMP-p300 signalling and induced by PPAR-alpha and FGF21 in starvation; these shifts have not been linked mechanistically to HMGCS2 protein abundance or ketogenic rate. Loss-of-function HMGCS2 mutations cause bouts of hypoketotic hypoglycaemia.

    Finding
    Malonyl-CoA inhibited ketogenesis from oleic acid but not from octanoic acid; the site of inhibition appeared to be the carnitine acyltransferase I reaction.
