Metabolism

The cell and its compartments

In brief

A thin fatty membrane surrounds each human cell, and inner membranes divide it into compartments with their own chemical reactions. A pump in the outer membrane spends energy from ATP to move sodium out and potassium in; potassium then leaks out and leaves the inside electrically negative. Hormones such as insulin bind receptors on the cell surface and start a chain of reactions that changes which genes are read.

Membranes and transport

A thin double layer of fatty molecules surrounds each cell, and two kinds of protein let ions cross it: channels and pumps 1.

An open channel passes tens of millions of ions per second down their electrochemical gradient 1. A pump has two gates that never open together, so it moves ions uphill several orders of magnitude more slowly 1. Pumps that hydrolyse ATP are called primary; secondary pumps couple the downhill flow of one ion, often Na+, to the uphill transport of another substrate 1.

The sodium pump

A pump in the outer membrane uses energy from ATP to move sodium out of the cell and potassium in 1.

In each cycle of the Na+/K+-ATPase, ATP phosphorylates a conserved aspartate, Asp376 in ATP1A1, and the pump exports 3 Na+ and imports 2 K+, up to a hundred times a second 12.

The Na+ gradient drives secondary pumps such as intestinal SGLT1, which takes up each glucose or galactose with 2 Na+ 2. The kidneys consume 7–10% of the body’s oxygen uptake and spend two-thirds or more of their share on the pump 3. A textbook table of rodent tissues in the standard state attributes to the pump 50–60% of oxygen consumption in brain, 5–10% in liver and skeletal muscle and 1–5% in heart 4.

Membrane potential

The inside of a resting cell is electrically negative, because potassium ions, far more concentrated inside, diffuse out 4.

K+, the dominant cation inside cells, is about 10 to 30 times more concentrated inside than outside, where Na+ dominates 4. K+ usually lies close to electrochemical equilibrium, where the inside-negative potential balances its concentration gradient 4. Resting potentials typically lie between −10 and −100 mV, depending on the ion channels a cell expresses 5. In pancreatic β cells, rising glucose raises ATP and lowers ADP, which closes ATP-sensitive K+ channels; the membrane depolarises, Ca2+ enters and insulin is released 5.

Membranes divide the cell interior into compartments, each with its own enzymes 6.

From receptor to gene

Insulin binds a receptor on the cell surface, and a chain of reactions transmits the signal to genes in the nucleus 11.

Through IRS proteins, phosphoinositide 3-kinase and PIP3, the receptor activates the kinase AKT 116. AKT phosphorylates the transcription factor FOXO1, which leaves the nucleus; transcription of the gluconeogenic genes G6PC1 and PCK1 falls 11122. Glucagon raises gluconeogenic gene transcription through cAMP, protein kinase A and the CREB–CBP–CRTC2 complex 12.

Unresolved questions

The textbook tissue shares derive from a 1997 review whose funding could not be determined; it attributes to the pump 19–28% of the oxygen consumption coupled to ATP synthesis in mammals in the standard state 134.

Under insulin clamps, mice lacking liver AKT1, AKT2 and FOXO1 still suppressed hepatic glucose production normally, through suppressed adipose lipolysis; the weight of this indirect route in humans remains to be explored 11.

    Palmer LG, Schnermann J. Integrated control of Na transport along the nephron Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2015.

    doi:10.2215/CJN.12391213PubMed 25098598

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    L.G. Palmer: National Institutes of Health grant R01-DK27847. J. Schnermann: Intramural Research Program of the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases.
    Finding
    Review. More than 500 g of sodium is filtered daily; under most conditions the kidneys excrete more than 95% of ingested sodium. The proximal tubule reabsorbs 60-70% (apical NHE3 the main mechanism), the ascending loop of Henle, mostly the thick limb, 25-30% (NKCC2 and NHE3), and the distal convoluted tubule and collecting ducts no more than 5-10%, through thiazide-sensitive NCC (early DCT) and amiloride-sensitive ENaC (late DCT, connecting tubule, collecting duct); distal reabsorption is the regulated part. Two-thirds or more of renal oxygen uptake is spent on the Na,K-ATPase. With plausible tenfold changes in intake, fractional reabsorption varies between about 97% and 99.7%. Sodium depletion increases ENaC activity, an effect mimicked by aldosterone. Potassium loading lowers NCC phosphorylation and abundance and raises ENaC activity, shifting sodium reabsorption to segments where principal cells secrete K+ in exchange for Na+. Changes in body Na content and blood volume are the likely cause of the relation between Na intake and blood pressure.

    Finding
    Expert-authored, peer-reviewed overview, posted 8 June 2000, comprehensive update 29 July 2021. More than 70 polypeptides form the respiratory chain on the inner mitochondrial membrane. Mitochondrial DNA encodes 13 essential respiratory-chain subunits (seven of complex I, one of complex III, three of complex IV and two of complex V), two ribosomal RNAs (12S and 16S) and 22 transfer RNAs for intra-mitochondrial protein synthesis; the remaining respiratory-chain polypeptides and the proteins for respiratory-chain assembly, mitochondrial structure and mtDNA maintenance and expression are encoded by the nuclear genome. Each human cell contains thousands of copies of mtDNA; in heteroplasmy, mutated and wild-type mtDNA coexist, and the proportion of mutated mtDNA must exceed a critical threshold before a cell expresses a biochemical defect of the respiratory chain. mtDNA pathogenic variants are transmitted by maternal inheritance; offspring of males with an mtDNA pathogenic variant do not inherit it.

    Teschke R. Alcoholic liver disease: alcohol metabolism, cascade of molecular mechanisms, cellular targets, and clinical aspects Biomedicines, 2018.

    doi:10.3390/biomedicines6040106PubMed 30424581

    Funding and finding
    Funding

    Finding
    Narrative review. In the liver, ADH (cytosol, NAD+), the microsomal ethanol-oxidizing system with CYP2E1 (endoplasmic reticulum, NADPH and O2) and catalase (peroxisomes, H2O2) oxidise ethanol to acetaldehyde; ADH and MEOS, but not catalase, are relevant in vivo. Km for ethanol: ADH 0.5 to 2.0 mM (animal studies), MEOS 7 to 11 mM, catalase 0.6 to 10 mM. MEOS is induced by chronic alcohol consumption through increased CYP2E1 synthesis. Mitochondrial ALDH oxidises acetaldehyde to acetate, which is released into the blood and oxidised in extrahepatic tissues; ADH and ALDH both generate NADH. The raised NADH/NAD+ ratio stimulates hepatic fatty acid synthesis and alpha-glycerophosphate supply, and acetaldehyde impairs mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation.

GNPTAB encodes the alpha and beta subunits of N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphotransferase, which synthesises the mannose 6-phosphate recognition marker that sorts newly synthesised lysosomal enzymes from the Golgi apparatus to lysosomes; the p.Lys4Gln variant impairs retention of the enzyme in the Golgi complex.     Finding
    Narrative review. INSR is a heterotetramer of two insulin-binding alpha subunits and two beta subunits with tyrosine kinase domains; trans-autophosphorylation of Tyr1162, Tyr1158 and Tyr1163 activates it, and it phosphorylates IRS1 and IRS2, which recruit PI3K (p85 regulatory, p110 catalytic). PIP3 recruits PDK1 and AKT, phosphorylated at Thr308 by PDK1 and at Ser473 by mTORC2. In primary human myotubes, knockdown of AKT2 but not AKT1 abrogated insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis; a partial loss-of-function AKT2 variant in about 1% of the Finnish population impairs insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and increases endogenous glucose production. AKT phosphorylation of TBC1D4 (AS160) blocks its inactivation of Rab GTPases and promotes GLUT4 storage-vesicle translocation, first reported in 1980; a truncating TBC1D4 mutation caused profound insulin resistance in one family. Glycogen synthesis accounts for about 75% of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal in human muscle; mice with GSK3 insensitive to insulin have normal insulin-stimulated glycogen synthesis, suggesting acute control mainly by glucose 6-phosphate allostery. Muscle stores energy mostly for its own use, releasing lactate and alanine. Hepatocyte glucose transport is not insulin-regulated; AKT phosphorylates FOXO1, excluding it from the nucleus and lowering G6pc and Pck1 transcription. Suppression of adipose lipolysis has an ED50 of about 20 µU/mL insulin, within a physiological range of about 5 to 60 µU/mL. Hepatic glycogen is nearly depleted after 48 h of fasting in humans, while gluconeogenic flux stays relatively constant for about 48 h. In rodents, acute insulin suppression of gluconeogenesis depends mainly on suppression of adipose lipolysis, hepatic acetyl-CoA and pyruvate carboxylase flux, and mice lacking hepatic AKT1, AKT2 and FOXO1 suppressed glucose production normally; the quantitative significance in humans remains to be explored. Insulin suppresses glucagon secretion through paracrine action in the islet.

    Petersen MC, Vatner DF, Shulman GI. Regulation of hepatic glucose metabolism in health and disease Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2017.

    doi:10.1038/nrendo.2017.80PubMed 28731034

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    M.C.P. acknowledges grant support from the US National Institutes of Health (NIH; grants F30 DK-104596 and T32 GM-007205). D.F.V. acknowledges grant support from the NIH (grant K23 DK-102874). G.I.S. acknowledges grant support from the NIH (grants R01 DK40936, R01 DK113984 and P30 DK045735).
    Finding
    Narrative review. Glucagon receptor (GCGR) activation raises cAMP and activates PKA, which inhibits L-type pyruvate kinase, phosphorylates PFK-2/FBPase-2 to favour its phosphatase activity and lower fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, an allosteric inhibitor of FBPase-1 (printed as fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 1), phosphorylates and inactivates glycogen synthase, and activates phosphorylase kinase, which phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase at Ser15; PKA also activates the CREB-CBP-CRTC2 complex for gluconeogenic gene transcription. FOXO proteins with PGC1-alpha raise PCK1 and G6PC expression and are excluded from the nucleus after AKT phosphorylation. Glucose enters hepatocytes by facilitated diffusion through GLUT2; glucose allosterically inhibits glycogen phosphorylase; glucose 6-phosphate is the key allosteric activator of GYS2. In overnight-fasted humans under somatostatin, hyperinsulinaemia was required to stimulate hepatic glycogen synthesis and hyperglycaemia to suppress glycogenolysis. Hepatic glucose production and storage are dysregulated in type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, and this imbalance contributes to hyperglycaemia in the fasted and postprandial states. Metformin is used as first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes and acts primarily through the suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis, by a molecular mechanism that remains under investigation. Several lines of evidence cast doubt on the necessity of AMPK: metformin continues to inhibit glucose production in Ampk-knockout primary mouse hepatocytes and improves glucose tolerance in Ampk-knockout mice to a similar extent as in wild-type mice. Inhibition of mitochondrial complex I is observed only at suprapharmacological, millimolar concentrations, in contrast to the clinically relevant plasma concentration range of 50 to 100 µM in patients who take 2 g of metformin daily. Metformin non-competitively inhibits mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase in isolated hepatocytes with a clinically relevant Ki of about 50 µM, which predicts impaired gluconeogenesis only from the redox-dependent substrates lactate and glycerol and not from pyruvate, alanine or dihydroxyacetone phosphate; the importance of the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle in gluconeogenesis in the human liver is unclear, so studies in humans are required.
