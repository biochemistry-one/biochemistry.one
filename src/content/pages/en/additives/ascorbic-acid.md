## What it is

Ascorbic acid is vitamin C, used in bakery wares, juices and drinks against oxidation and to treat flour [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid; @nhc-2024-gb2760].

EFSA assessed E300 (INS 300) with sodium ascorbate (E301) and calcium ascorbate (E302) [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid].

## How often it is used

Ascorbic acid is the fifth most frequent additive in French packaged foods: 7,919 of 126,556 products in 2019 [@chazelas-2020-additives].

Of 106,489 French adults, 80.56% consumed it, a mean 15.85 mg/day [@chazelas-2021-mixtures].

## In the body

The gut absorbs small doses almost fully, large doses less well; the kidneys excrete the excess [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid].

Sodium-dependent transport absorbs up to 98% of low doses, 75% of 1 g and 16% of 12 g [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid]. Oxidised ascorbate is reduced back, or degraded irreversibly to mainly oxalate and L-threonate [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid]. After injected labelled ascorbate, 44% of urinary radioactivity was recovered as oxalate [@efsa-2013-vitamin-c]. At high doses, intestinal bacteria break down unabsorbed vitamin C [@efsa-2013-vitamin-c].

## What studies show

Vitamin C helps iron absorption, and high-dose supplements were linked to kidney stones in men [@efsa-2009-vitamin-c-claims; @thomas-2013-kidney-stones].

Ascorbate keeps non-haem iron ferrous and raises its absorption 1.5- to 10-fold [@efsa-2009-vitamin-c-claims]. In [nitrite](/additives/sodium-nitrite/)-cured meat it can lower nitrosamine levels except NDMA, NPYR, NPIP and NSAR [@mortensen-2017-nitrite].

Over a median 11.3 to 11.7 years, stone hazard ratios for total vitamin C of 1,000 mg/day or more, against under 90 mg/day, were 1.43 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.79) in men and 0.99 (0.90 to 1.09) in women [@ferraro-2016-kidney-stones]. Over 11 years, Swedish men taking ascorbic acid supplements alone had 310 first stones per 100,000 person-years, non-users 163 (relative risk 1.92, 1.33 to 2.77) [@thomas-2013-kidney-stones].

With copper or iron ions, ascorbic and benzoic acids can yield traces of benzene, a carcinogen [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid]. EFSA found no adverse effects in animals and judged a numerical acceptable daily intake unnecessary [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid].

:::unresolved
EFSA saw a strong indication that intake below 1,500 mg/day is not a stone risk factor, although in a cohort it cited the relative risk in men at 1,000 mg/day or more was 1.41 (1.11 to 1.80) [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid]. Crude counts in men were 260 stones in 81,316 person-years against 157 in 45,363; the excess was seen only after adjustment, chiefly for potassium [@ferraro-2016-kidney-stones]. EFSA judged pro-oxidant effects with metal ions, seen in vitro, unconfirmed in vivo [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid].
:::

## Regulation

Major jurisdictions permit ascorbic acid, mostly without numerical limits [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid; @fda-2026-ascorbic-acid; @mhlw-2024-jsfa; @nhc-2024-gb2760].

| Jurisdiction | Rule |
|---|---|
| European Union | Quantum satis in most foods (2015) [@efsa-2015-ascorbic-acid] |
| United States | Generally recognised as safe, as preservative and nutrient [@fda-2026-ascorbic-acid] |
| Japan | Designated additive [@mhlw-2024-jsfa] |
| China | Quantum satis outside 59 food categories; 0.2 g/kg in wheat flour [@nhc-2024-gb2760] |
