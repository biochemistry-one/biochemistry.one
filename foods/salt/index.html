Foods

Salt

In brief

Table salt consists mainly of sodium chloride, and iodised salt also supplies iodine, a component of thyroid hormones. The kidneys filter far more sodium each day than a person eats and return almost all of it to the blood. Eating less sodium lowers blood pressure, and in a large trial in rural China a salt substitute containing potassium lowered the rates of stroke and death.

What it is

Table salt consists mainly of sodium chloride 1.

Per 100 g, US iodised table salt averages 38.7 g sodium and 5080 µg iodine 2.

In the body

The kidneys filter over 500 g of sodium a day and return almost all of it to the blood 3.

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase exports 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺ per ATP hydrolysed 4. It powers tubular sodium reabsorption and accounts for two-thirds or more of renal oxygen uptake 3. The proximal tubule reabsorbs 60–70% of filtered sodium (mainly NHE3), the thick ascending limb 25–30% (NKCC2, NHE3) and the distal nephron at most 5–10% (NCC, ENaC), which matches excretion to intake 3.

Renin, released when afferent arteriolar pressure or distal tubular sodium chloride falls, cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; angiotensin-converting enzyme then forms angiotensin II 1.

Angiotensin II stimulates proximal sodium reabsorption, arteriolar constriction and aldosterone release; aldosterone stimulates distal sodium recovery in exchange for potassium 1.

The sodium–iodide symporter (SLC5A5), driven by the Na⁺ gradient of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, moves one I⁻ with two Na⁺ into thyroid cells 5.

What studies show

Eating less sodium lowers blood pressure 6.

In DASH-Sodium, 412 adults were fed a typical US or DASH diet at three sodium levels, 30 days each 6. From high to low sodium, systolic pressure fell by 6.7 mm Hg on the typical diet and 3.0 mm Hg on DASH 6.

In SSaSS, 600 rural Chinese villages (20,995 people with prior stroke, or aged 60 or more with poorly controlled blood pressure) were randomised to a 75% sodium chloride, 25% potassium chloride substitute or regular salt 7. Over a mean 4.74 years, stroke rates per 1000 person-years were 29.14 against 33.65, 4.51 fewer (rate ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.96) 7. Per 1000 person-years, death rates were 39.28 against 44.61 (0.88, 0.82 to 0.95), and rates of serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalaemia 3.35 against 3.30 7. Australia’s National Health and Medical Research Council funded the trial; a manufacturer donated the substitute in years 3–4 7.

In a Cochrane review of six controlled studies, iodine status improved with iodised salt in most, and goitre tended to decrease 8.

Industry-funded results

In PURE, 101,945 adults in 17 countries were followed for a mean 3.7 years; sodium excretion was estimated from one morning urine sample 9. Death or major cardiovascular events occurred in 4.3% of 10,810 below 3.00 g/day, 3.1% of 46,663 at 4.00–5.99 g/day (reference) and 3.3% of 11,017 at 7.00 g/day or more (adjusted odds ratios 1.27 and 1.15) 9. Funders included seven pharmaceutical companies, the Unilever Health Institute and the South Africa Sugar Association; the authors report no funder role 9.

Consensus statements

WHO strongly recommends less than 2 g sodium (5 g salt) a day for adults 10. It conditionally suggests potassium-containing substitutes for table salt, except in children, pregnancy and impaired potassium excretion 11. It recommends iodising all food-grade salt, judging this compatible with salt reduction 12.

Unresolved questions

PURE’s J-shaped association is disputed 913. In TOHP (2974 adults, 272 deaths), sodium averaged over up to seven 24-hour urines was related to death linearly over a median 24 years (hazard ratio 1.12 per 1000 mg/day, 1.00 to 1.26) 13. With Kawasaki-formula estimates from the same urines, intake was overestimated by 1297 mg/day and the relation appeared J-shaped 14.

People at known risk from extra potassium were excluded from all 26 salt-substitute trials in a Cochrane review 15. Child development and mortality were not assessed in the iodised-salt studies 8.

    EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Turck D, Castenmiller J, de Henauw S, Hirsch-Ernst KI, Kearney J, et al. Dietary reference values for sodium EFSA Journal, 2019.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2019.5778PubMed 32626425

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (question number EFSA-Q-2011-01224).
    Finding
    Sodium chloride is the main constituent of table salt; 1 g of sodium chloride provides 0.4 g of sodium and 0.6 g of chloride. In healthy people almost all dietary sodium is absorbed, along most of the small and large intestine; apical co-transporters couple sodium entry to the uptake of solutes, the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase exchanges three intracellular Na+ for two extracellular K+, net sodium and water absorption occurs in the distal ileum and colon, and distal absorption responds to aldosterone. Renin is released from the juxtaglomerular apparatus in response to reduced afferent arteriolar pressure, increased sympathetic activity and decreased distal tubular NaCl; renin hydrolyses angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme converts to angiotensin II; angiotensin II stimulates proximal sodium reabsorption, arteriolar constriction and adrenal aldosterone release; aldosterone stimulates sodium recovery in exchange for potassium in distal tubule, collecting ducts and distal colon. Genotypes with increased ENaC function cause volume expansion, hypertension and hypokalaemia (e.g. Liddle's syndrome). A sodium intake of 2.0 g/day is considered safe and adequate for adults, including pregnant and lactating women.

  3. Independent research

    Palmer LG, Schnermann J. Integrated control of Na transport along the nephron Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2015.

    doi:10.2215/CJN.12391213PubMed 25098598

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    L.G. Palmer: National Institutes of Health grant R01-DK27847. J. Schnermann: Intramural Research Program of the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases.
    Finding
    Review. More than 500 g of sodium is filtered daily; under most conditions the kidneys excrete more than 95% of ingested sodium. The proximal tubule reabsorbs 60-70% (apical NHE3 the main mechanism), the ascending loop of Henle, mostly the thick limb, 25-30% (NKCC2 and NHE3), and the distal convoluted tubule and collecting ducts no more than 5-10%, through thiazide-sensitive NCC (early DCT) and amiloride-sensitive ENaC (late DCT, connecting tubule, collecting duct); distal reabsorption is the regulated part. Two-thirds or more of renal oxygen uptake is spent on the Na,K-ATPase. With plausible tenfold changes in intake, fractional reabsorption varies between about 97% and 99.7%. Sodium depletion increases ENaC activity, an effect mimicked by aldosterone. Potassium loading lowers NCC phosphorylation and abundance and raises ENaC activity, shifting sodium reabsorption to segments where principal cells secrete K+ in exchange for Na+. Changes in body Na content and blood volume are the likely cause of the relation between Na intake and blood pressure.

  10. Expert consensus

    World Health Organization. Guideline: sodium intake for adults and children World Health Organization, 2012.

    PubMed 23658998

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    WHO acknowledges financial support from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of the Government of Japan, the Korea Food and Drug Administration, the Korea Health Industry Development Institute and the International Kidney Evaluation Association Japan.
    Finding
    WHO recommends a reduction in sodium intake to reduce blood pressure and risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke and coronary heart disease in adults, and a reduction to less than 2 g/day sodium (5 g/day salt) in adults (strong recommendation); in children, a reduction to control blood pressure, with the adult maximum adjusted downward for energy requirements (strong recommendation).

  11. Expert consensus

    World Health Organization. Use of lower-sodium salt substitutes: WHO guideline World Health Organization, 2025.

    PubMed 39946522

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    WHO acknowledges financial support from Resolve to Save Lives and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of the Government of Japan for the guideline development work, and Qingdao University for hosting a guideline group meeting.
    Finding
    If choosing to use table salt, WHO suggests replacing regular table salt with lower-sodium salt substitutes that contain potassium (conditional recommendation), for adults in general populations, not pregnant women or children, and excluding individuals with kidney impairments or other conditions that might compromise potassium excretion. The recommendation applies to discretionary table salt, not to salt in manufactured or restaurant foods, and accompanies the strong recommendation to reduce sodium intake to less than 2 g/day.

  12. Expert consensus

    World Health Organization. Guideline: fortification of food-grade salt with iodine for the prevention and control of iodine deficiency disorders World Health Organization, 2014.

    PubMed 25473709

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    WHO thanks the Government of Luxembourg and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation for financial support; the Micronutrient Initiative supports the WHO Evidence and Programme Guidance Unit for the commissioning of systematic reviews.
    Finding
    All food-grade salt used in household and food processing should be fortified with iodine (strong recommendation); evidence quality moderate for goitre and cretinism, low for cognitive function. Suggested iodine addition ranges from 65 mg/kg salt at an intake of 3 g salt/day to 39 mg/kg at 5 g/day, 20 mg/kg at 10 g/day and 14 mg/kg at 14 g/day, based on 150 µg iodine/day, 30% losses before consumption and 92% bioavailability. Salt iodisation and reduction of salt to less than 5 g/day are compatible.
