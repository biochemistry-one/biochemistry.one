Foods

Sugar and fructose

In brief

Table sugar is half glucose and half fructose. The liver takes up most dietary fructose and converts part of it to fat, and fructose breakdown raises uric acid. Trials show that sugary drinks increase weight gain, and documents show that a sugar-industry group paid for a 1967 review that played down the risks of sugar.

What it is

Table sugar, or sucrose, is made of glucose and fructose in equal parts 1. Glucose–fructose syrup, called high-fructose corn syrup in the United States, is made from starch and mostly contains 42% or 55% free fructose 2. Free sugars include sugars added to foods and those in honey, syrups and fruit juices 2.

In the body

The liver takes up most dietary fructose and breaks it down by a route bypassing the main control point of glucose breakdown 12.

Ketohexokinase C, aldolase B and triokinase convert fructose to triose phosphates, which enter glycolysis below phosphofructokinase, the step inhibited by ATP and citrate, and yield fatty acids 12.

Large fructose loads transiently lower hepatic ATP and phosphate; falling phosphate activates AMP deaminase, and AMP is degraded to uric acid 12.

Loss of ketohexokinase causes benign essential fructosuria, and loss of aldolase B hereditary fructose intolerance 13.

What studies show

In a double-blind trial in 641 Dutch children, a daily sugar-free drink led to less weight gain over 18 months than one with 26 g sucrose 4.

Weight rose by 6.35 kg and 7.37 kg (difference −1.01 kg, 95% CI −1.54 to −0.48) 4. In 224 overweight or obese adolescents, a 1-year intervention including home delivery of non-caloric drinks reduced weight gain at 1 year by 1.9 kg; BMI change at 2 years, the primary outcome, did not differ (P = 0.46) 5.

In 32 overweight or obese adults given drinks supplying 25% of energy requirements as fructose or glucose for 10 weeks, weight rose similarly, but only fructose raised postprandial fractional hepatic de novo lipogenesis (11.4% to 16.9%), intra-abdominal fat (+14.0%) and fasting apoB (+27.2%) 6. Groups were matched, and the US National Institutes of Health funded the study 6. In a randomised trial in 94 young men, 80 g a day of fructose or sucrose in drinks for 7 weeks doubled the fractional secretion rate of newly synthesised VLDL palmitate (median 19.7 and 20.8 against 9.1%/day without drinks); glucose did not (11.0%/day) 7.

The Sugar Research Foundation funded a review in 1965, set its objective and received drafts; in the review, published in 1967 without disclosure of this role, studies linking sucrose to coronary heart disease were discounted 8. Evidence linking sugary drinks to weight gain was judged insufficient in 5 of 6 conclusions of systematic reviews with food-industry conflicts of interest and in 2 of 12 without (relative risk 5.0, 95% CI 1.3 to 19.3) 9.

Industry-funded results

In 46,393 US men followed for 12 years, 2 or more servings of sugar-sweetened soft drinks a day, against less than 1 a month, were associated with gout (16 cases in 7392 person-years against 279 in 158,891; relative risk 1.85, 95% CI 1.08 to 3.16); TAP Pharmaceuticals co-funded the study 10.

Consensus statements

WHO strongly recommends free sugars below 10% of energy, on moderate-quality evidence about dental caries, and conditionally below 5% 11. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) could not set a tolerable upper intake level and advises intake of added and free sugars as low as possible within a nutritionally adequate diet 2.

Unresolved questions

Effects of fructose beyond its energy are unsettled: in trials reviewed by EFSA, effects of free fructose and glucose on weight, liver fat and blood lipids did not appear to differ, but fructose appeared to raise hepatic insulin resistance and uric acid more 2. Large long-term trials of added sugars on cardiometabolic risk factors are lacking 1.

References

  1. Independent research

    Hannou SA, Haslam DE, McKeown NM, Herman MA. Fructose metabolism and metabolic disease Journal of Clinical Investigation, 2018.

    doi:10.1172/JCI96702PubMed 29388924

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    This work is supported by American Heart Association 16CSA28590003 (to MAH and NMM), NIH R01DK100425 (to MAH), NIH 5T32HL069772-15 (to DEH), and US Department of Agriculture Agricultural Research Service agreement 58-1950-4-003 (to NMM).
    Finding
    Narrative review. Sucrose is composed of equal portions of glucose and fructose. GLUT5 (SLC2A5) absorbs fructose with a Km of 6 mM; healthy adults absorb from less than 5 g to more than 50 g of free fructose. The liver extracts about 70% of an oral fructose load and 15% to 30% of an oral glucose load; the intestine metabolises up to 30% of an oral fructose load. KHK phosphorylates fructose to fructose 1-phosphate; the C isoform (liver, kidney, intestine) has a Km of 0.8 mM, the A isoform 8 mM. ALDOB cleaves fructose 1-phosphate to dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde, which TKFC phosphorylates to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, so fructose carbon enters below phosphofructokinase. Fructose activates ChREBP and SREBP1c; malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT1A. Falling intracellular phosphate activates AMP deaminase and AMP is catabolised to uric acid; Mendelian randomisation studies do not strongly support a causal role for circulating uric acid in cardiometabolic disease. KHK loss of function causes essential fructosuria with no documented adverse effects. Large long-term randomised trials of added sugars on cardiometabolic risk are lacking.

  2. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Turck D, Bohn T, Castenmiller J, de Henauw S, Hirsch-Ernst KI, et al. Tolerable upper intake level for dietary sugars EFSA Journal, 2022.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2022.7074PubMed 35251356

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority. Requestor: European Commission, following a request from the national food competent authorities of Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden (question EFSA-Q-2016-00414).
    Finding
    Systematic reviews of 49 RCTs and 66 cohorts on metabolic endpoints. Added sugars are mono- and disaccharides added during processing or preparation or at the table; free sugars are added sugars plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices and juice concentrates. Sucrase-isomaltase hydrolyses sucrose; glucose enters enterocytes by SGLT1, fructose by facilitated diffusion through GLUT5, incompletely and enhanced by co-ingested glucose; GLUT2 moves all three monosaccharides into portal blood. Hepatic glycolysis is restrained at phosphofructokinase by ATP and citrate, fructolysis is not; almost all fructose entering hepatocytes becomes triose phosphates, from which lactate, glucose, glycogen, glycerol and fatty acids are formed. Large fructose loads transiently deplete hepatic ATP, with AMP formation and purine degradation. Dietary sugars are a well-established hazard for dental caries: plaque bacteria convert sugars to organic acids that demineralise enamel and dentine, and about 30% of plaque is matrix largely of glucans made from sucrose by bacterial glucosyltransferases. Certainty of a causal relationship for added and free sugars (RCTs on surrogate endpoints): moderate for obesity and dyslipidaemia, low for NAFLD and type 2 diabetes, very low for hypertension; too few RCTs below 10% of energy. Cohorts did not support a relationship of dietary sugars in isocaloric exchange with chronic metabolic disease. In RCTs, free fructose and free glucose had similar effects on body weight, liver fat, glucose tolerance, blood lipids and blood pressure, while free fructose increased hepatic insulin resistance and uric acid more; certainty for fructose and gout moderate (cohorts). Sugar-sweetened beverages: high certainty for obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease, moderate for gout. Glucose-fructose syrups are made by hydrolysing starch to glucose and converting about half enzymatically to fructose; most contain 42% fructose (processed foods) or 55% (sugar-sweetened beverages), as free monosaccharides; called high-fructose corn syrup in the United States and isoglucose in the EU. No tolerable upper intake level could be set; intake of added and free sugars should be as low as possible in a nutritionally adequate diet.

    Initial posting 17 December 2015; last update 18 February 2021. Autosomal recessive; biallelic pathogenic ALDOB variants.     Kearns CE, Schmidt LA, Glantz SA. Sugar Industry and Coronary Heart Disease Research: A Historical Analysis of Internal Industry Documents JAMA Internal Medicine, 2016.

    doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.5394PubMed 27617709

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    This work was supported by the UCSF Philip R. Lee Institute for Health Policy Studies, a donation by the Hellmann Family Fund to the UCSF Center for Tobacco Control Research and Education, the UCSF School of Dentistry Department of Orofacial Sciences and Global Oral Health Program, National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research grant DE-007306 and National Cancer Institute Grant CA-087472. The funders had no role in design and conduct of the study; collection, management, analysis, and interpretation of the data; and preparation, review, or approval of the manuscript.
    Finding
    In 1965 the Sugar Research Foundation approved Project 226, a literature review by Hegsted and McGandy overseen by Stare, and paid $6500 ($48,900 in 2016 dollars). The foundation set the review's objective, contributed articles for inclusion and received drafts. The two-part review, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1967, discounted studies linking sucrose to coronary heart disease and concluded that reducing dietary cholesterol and substituting polyunsaturated for saturated fat was the only dietary intervention needed; the foundation's funding and role were not disclosed. The authors state that evidence of industry influence on the review's conclusions is circumstantial. In 1971 the industry influenced the National Caries Program of the National Institute of Dental Research to shift its emphasis to interventions other than restricting sucrose.

  11. Expert consensus

    World Health Organization. Guideline: Sugars Intake for Adults and Children World Health Organization, Geneva, 2015.

    PubMed 25905159

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    WHO expresses special appreciation to the following for providing financial support for the guideline development work: the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of the Government of Japan; the Korean Food and Drug Administration, through grants to the Korea Health Industry Development Institute; Zhejiang University in Hangzhou, China; and the WHO Regional Office for Europe.
    Finding
    WHO recommends a reduced intake of free sugars throughout the lifecourse (strong recommendation) and, in adults and children, reducing free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake (strong recommendation); WHO suggests a further reduction to below 5% (conditional recommendation). The 10% limit rests on moderate-quality evidence from observational studies of dental caries and the 5% limit on very-low-quality evidence from ecological studies. The excess body weight associated with free sugars results from excess energy intake.
