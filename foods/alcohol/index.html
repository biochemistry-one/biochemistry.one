Foods

Alcohol

In brief

The alcohol in drinks is ethanol, which the liver converts to acetaldehyde, a compound that damages DNA, and then to acetate. A gene variant common in East Asia slows the second step, so carriers flush when they drink, and carriers who drink have a higher risk of oesophageal cancer. Studies that seemed to show longer lives in light drinkers mostly counted former drinkers as non-drinkers, and analyses that correct for this find no significant survival benefit.

What it is

The alcohol in drinks is ethanol, formed when glucose is fermented 1, absorbed mainly from the small intestine and distributed throughout body water 2.

In the body

The liver converts ethanol to acetaldehyde, which damages DNA, and acetaldehyde to acetate 23.

Class I alcohol dehydrogenases catalyse most hepatic ethanol oxidation; ADH1B*2 (rs1229984) has a Vmax of 190 min⁻¹ in vitro against 5.2 min⁻¹ for ADH1B*1, yet carriers of European descent eliminate ethanol only about 14% faster 2. The CYP2E1-dependent microsomal system, with a Km for ethanol of 7–11 mM against 0.5–2.0 mM for ADH in animal studies, is induced by chronic drinking 1.

Mitochondrial ALDH2 oxidises acetaldehyde with a Km of 0.2 µM, and a single ALDH2*2 subunit (rs671, Glu504Lys) renders the tetramer nearly inactive; the allele is common in East Asia and rare elsewhere 2. In carriers, blood acetaldehyde can reach 30–75 µM, against 3 µM or less in non-carriers, causing flushing, nausea, headache and tachycardia 2.

Both steps reduce NAD⁺ to NADH, and the raised hepatic NADH/NAD⁺ ratio stimulates fatty acid synthesis 1. Ethanol given with an adequate diet produced hepatic fat accumulation in all five previously abstinent alcoholics 4.

What studies show

Drinkers with the flushing variant have a much higher risk of oesophageal cancer than other drinkers 3. In Japanese and Taiwanese case-control studies, heterozygous drinkers had odds ratios of 3.7 to 18.1 for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma after adjustment for intake; in non-drinkers, ALDH2 deficiency does not alter the risk 3.

Former drinkers, whose relative risk of death was 1.22 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.31), were counted as abstainers in 65 of 87 mortality cohorts 5. Unadjusted, the pooled curve was J-shaped: relative risk 0.86 (0.83 to 0.90) at 1.3–24.9 g ethanol a day, against 0.97 (0.88 to 1.07) after adjustment for abstainer biases and study characteristics 5. In an update of 107 cohorts, fully adjusted relative risks against lifetime non-drinkers were 0.93 (0.85 to 1.01) at 1.3–24.0 g a day and 1.35 (1.23 to 1.47) at 65 g or more 6.

Industry-funded results

In the China Kadoorie Biobank (512,715 adults, follow-up about 10 years), men’s mean intake was 3, 37 and 157 g a week with rs671 AA, AG and GG 7. Self-reported intake had U-shaped associations with stroke; genotype-predicted intake did not; per 280 g a week, its relative risks were 1.38 (1.26 to 1.51) for stroke and 0.96 (0.78 to 1.18) for myocardial infarction 7. Its funders, GlaxoSmithKline among them, had no role, according to the paper 7.

Consensus statements

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies alcohol consumption, ethanol in alcoholic beverages and the associated acetaldehyde as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1); alcohol consumption causes cancers of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, colorectum, liver and female breast 8.

Unresolved questions

Whether alcohol protects the heart is unresolved 9. In 599,912 current drinkers (median follow-up 7.5 years), the hazard ratio for myocardial infarction was 0.94 (0.91 to 0.97) per 100 g a week 10; in Chinese men, genotype-predicted intake was not associated with it 7. In 2018 the US National Institutes of Health ended MACH, a trial of one drink a day against none, after its reviewers found that officials had sought industry funding inappropriately; five alcohol producers provided about two thirds of the $100 million 9.

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    There was no funding of this article.
    Finding
    Narrative review. In the liver, ADH (cytosol, NAD+), the microsomal ethanol-oxidizing system with CYP2E1 (endoplasmic reticulum, NADPH and O2) and catalase (peroxisomes, H2O2) oxidise ethanol to acetaldehyde; ADH and MEOS, but not catalase, are relevant in vivo. Km for ethanol: ADH 0.5 to 2.0 mM (animal studies), MEOS 7 to 11 mM, catalase 0.6 to 10 mM. MEOS is induced by chronic alcohol consumption through increased CYP2E1 synthesis. Mitochondrial ALDH oxidises acetaldehyde to acetate, which is released into the blood and oxidised in extrahepatic tissues; ADH and ALDH both generate NADH. The raised NADH/NAD+ ratio stimulates hepatic fatty acid synthesis and alpha-glycerophosphate supply, and acetaldehyde impairs mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation.

    Finding
    Alcohol consumption, ethanol in alcoholic beverages and acetaldehyde associated with the consumption of alcoholic beverages are carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). Alcohol consumption causes cancers of the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, colorectum, liver (hepatocellular carcinoma) and female breast. Acetaldehyde associated with alcohol consumption causes cancers of the oesophagus and of the upper aerodigestive tract combined. Sufficient epidemiological evidence shows that humans deficient in the oxidation of acetaldehyde to acetate have a substantially increased risk of alcohol-related cancers, in particular of the oesophagus and the upper aerodigestive tract.

    Finding
    The National Institutes of Health stopped the worldwide MACH trial in 2018, announcing its end in June 2018, after an NIH Advisory Committee to the Director working group review. Approximately two thirds of the funding for this $100 million trial was provided by five global alcohol producers (Anheuser-Busch InBev, Carlsberg, Diageo, Heineken and Pernod Ricard) through the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health.
