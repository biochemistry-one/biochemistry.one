Foods

Coffee

In brief

Coffee supplies caffeine and plant acids called chlorogenic acids, and coffee brewed without a paper filter also supplies cafestol, which raises LDL cholesterol. The liver breaks down caffeine mainly with one enzyme, CYP1A2, whose activity is higher in smokers. Coffee drinkers die at slightly lower rates in large studies, but gene variants linked to drinking more coffee are not linked to a lower death rate.

What it is

Brewed coffee supplies caffeine and chlorogenic acids, and unfiltered coffee also cafestol, which raises cholesterol 123.

A 237 g cup contains about 95 mg caffeine 1. Filter coffee averages 70 mg of 5-caffeoylquinic acid, or chlorogenic acid, per 100 ml 2. Cafetière coffee, 0.9 litres a day, supplied 38 mg of the diterpene cafestol, paper-filtered coffee less than 1 mg 3.

In the body

Caffeine is absorbed completely, blocks adenosine receptors in the brain and is broken down in the liver, mainly by one enzyme, CYP1A2 4.

CYP1A2 performs over 95% of primary caffeine metabolism, mostly N3-demethylation to paraxanthine, 70% to 80% of a dose 5. The half-life is about 4 hours (range 2 to 8), and 6 to 16 hours in pregnancy, when CYP1A2 activity falls 4.

Smoking induces CYP1A2 and raises caffeine clearance up to 1.72-fold 4. In two studies, CYP1A2 activity was higher with the −163AA genotype (rs762551) in smokers only 4. Caffeine antagonises adenosine A1 and A2A receptors 4.

What studies show

Unfiltered coffee raises LDL cholesterol; coffee drinkers die at slightly lower rates, but gene variants that raise coffee intake are not associated with lower mortality 367.

In a randomised trial in 46 adults, 24 weeks of cafetière coffee raised LDL cholesterol by 0.26 mmol/L (95% CI 0.04 to 0.47) against filtered coffee 3.

In UK Biobank, 14,225 of 498,134 adults died over a median 7 years; against non-drinkers, the adjusted hazard ratio at 2 to 3 cups a day was 0.88 (0.84 to 0.93), similar for decaffeinated coffee and across genetic caffeine-metabolism scores 6. In Copenhagen, coffee intake had a U-shaped association with death, but alleles near AHR and CYP1A1/2 that raise intake were not associated with it (hazard ratio per allele 1.01, 0.99 to 1.03; 12,656 deaths among 112,509) 7.

Among 2635 pregnant women in England, fetal growth restriction occurred in 10.9% below 100 mg caffeine a day and 18.2% at 300 mg or more (adjusted odds ratio 1.4, 1.0 to 2.0); tea supplied 62% of the caffeine 8.

Industry-funded results

In a non-randomised trial funded by the Institute for Scientific Information on Coffee (ISIC), a consortium of European coffee companies, 47 coffee drinkers had 12% higher total and 7% higher HDL cholesterol after a month at 8 cups of filtered coffee a day than after a month without; LDL cholesterol did not change significantly, and the authors called the HDL effect beneficial 9. The paper reports no funder role in design or analysis 9.

Consensus statements

EFSA judged habitual caffeine intake up to 400 mg a day of no safety concern for non-pregnant adults and, for the fetus, up to 200 mg a day in pregnancy; it treated as causal a cohort risk judged clinically relevant from about 200 mg 4. IARC classed drinking coffee as not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity (Group 3); observers for ISIC and the US National Coffee Association took no part in the evaluation 10.

Unresolved questions

Cohort and genetic mortality findings conflict. In Copenhagen, 36% of those drinking over 5 cups a day smoked, against 12% of non-drinkers, and the genetic analysis lacked power to exclude the cohort estimate 7. Genetically predicted maternal coffee intake was not associated with miscarriage, stillbirth or preterm birth 11.

How cafestol raises LDL cholesterol in humans is unestablished; it activates FXR and PXR in cell assays and represses hepatic Cyp7a1, rate-limiting in bile acid synthesis, in wild-type mice without raising their cholesterol 12.

  4. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA). Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine EFSA Journal, 2015.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Question No EFSA-Q-2013-00220), adopted on 23 April 2015.
    Finding
    Caffeine is rapidly (tmax 30-120 minutes) and completely absorbed and crosses the blood-brain and placental barriers. N-3 demethylation to paraxanthine by hepatic CYP1A2 is the main route (70-80%); CYP1A2 accounts for about 95% of caffeine clearance. Plasma half-life about 4 hours (range about 2 to 8). In a study of saliva caffeine clearance, smoking increased clearance 1.22-, 1.47-, 1.66- and 1.72-fold for 1-5, 6-10, 11-20 and more than 20 cigarettes a day, and each litre of daily coffee 1.45-fold; CYP1A2 induction in smokers is reported; CYP1A2 activity was higher in smokers with the -163AA genotype (CYP1A2*1F, rs762551) than with AC or CC, without differences in non-smokers. In pregnancy the half-life increased to 6-16 hours, and at the end of pregnancy is three to four times longer than in the non-pregnant state; CYP1A2 activity was progressively lower than post partum, by 65.2% at weeks 36-40. The fetus and placenta cannot metabolise caffeine. Caffeine acts as an antagonist at adenosine A1 and A2A receptors and is a non-specific phosphodiesterase inhibitor with a Ki of 48 µM, the peak concentration after a 450 mg dose. Habitual intake up to 400 mg a day raises no safety concern for non-pregnant adults; up to 200 mg a day in pregnancy raises no safety concern for the fetus, based on two prospective cohorts (CARE Study Group 2008; Sengpiel 2013) showing a dose-dependent association with fetal growth restriction and small for gestational age, observed at all levels of intake with no threshold and judged clinically relevant at about 200 mg; the Panel noted that cohorts cannot provide evidence of causation but assumed the relationship causal for the assessment.

  5. Textbook or database

    Thorn CF, Aklillu E, McDonagh EM, Klein TE, Altman RB. PharmGKB summary: caffeine pathway Pharmacogenetics and Genomics, 2012.

    doi:10.1097/FPC.0b013e3283505d5ePubMed 22293536

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    This work was supported by the NIH/NIGMS (R24 GM61374).
    Finding
    Caffeine is almost completely metabolised, 3% or less being excreted unchanged in urine. The main route, 70% to 80% of the dose, is N3-demethylation to paraxanthine by hepatic CYP1A2; 1-N-demethylation to theobromine and 7-N-demethylation to theophylline each account for about 7% to 8%, and C-8 hydroxylation to 1,3,7-trimethyluric acid for the remaining 15%. CYP1A2 is responsible for more than 95% of the primary metabolism of caffeine, which is therefore used as a probe drug for CYP1A2 activity. The secondary metabolism of paraxanthine to 1-methylxanthine, 1-methyluric acid, 5-acetylamino-6-formylamino-3-methyluracil and 1,7-dimethyluric acid is carried out by CYP1A2, CYP2A6, N-acetyltransferase 2 and xanthine dehydrogenase. CYP2C8, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 also take part in primary metabolism, with lower intrinsic clearance than CYP1A2.

    14,225 deaths occurred. Adjustment for detailed smoking history had the largest effect on estimates; participation was about 5.5%.

  7. Independent research

    Nordestgaard AT, Nordestgaard BG. Coffee intake, cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: observational and Mendelian randomization analyses in 95 000-223 000 individuals International Journal of Epidemiology, 2016.

    doi:10.1093/ije/dyw325PubMed 28031317

    Funding and finding
    Participants
    112,509
    Follow-up
    median 6.0 years for all-cause mortality; to November 2014
    Funding
    This work was supported by Herlev and Gentofte Hospital, Copenhagen University Hospital and the Danish Heart Foundation. The funding organizations had no role in the design and conduct of the study, the collection, analysis and interpretation of the data or in the writing of the paper.
    Finding
    Observationally, among 95,366 people with 5422 deaths, coffee intake had U-shaped associations with ischaemic heart disease, ischaemic stroke and all-cause mortality, lowest at medium intake; current smokers were 12% of non-drinkers and 36% of those drinking more than 5 cups a day. A caffeine intake allele score (variants near AHR and CYP1A1/2) was associated with 42% higher coffee intake from 0 to 4 alleles (2.2 against 3.1 cups a day; R2 0.009). Among 112,509 people with 12,656 deaths, hazard ratios per allele were 1.01 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.03) for all-cause mortality, 1.02 (0.99 to 1.06) for cardiovascular mortality and 1.02 (1.00 to 1.03) for ischaemic heart disease. With 80% power the genetic analysis could exclude a per-allele odds ratio of 0.97; about 225,000 cases and 225,000 controls would be needed to exclude the observational hazard ratio of 0.86 for 4 to 5 against 0 cups a day.

  8. Independent research

    CARE Study Group. Maternal caffeine intake during pregnancy and risk of fetal growth restriction: a large prospective observational study BMJ, 2008.

    doi:10.1136/bmj.a2332PubMed 18981029

    Funding and finding
    Participants
    2,635
    Follow-up
    From 8 to 12 weeks of pregnancy to delivery; recruitment September 2003 to June 2006
    Funding
    Food Standards Agency, United Kingdom, Grant contract No T01032/33. The steering group included Food Standards Agency staff.
    Finding
    Mean caffeine intake in pregnancy was 159 mg a day; tea supplied 62%, coffee 14%, cola 12% and chocolate 8%. Fetal growth restriction (birth weight below the 10th customised centile) occurred in 122 of 1122 women below 100 mg a day, 90 of 691 at 100-199 mg, 63 of 376 at 200-299 mg and 63 of 347 at 300 mg or more. Odds ratios adjusted for salivary cotinine and alcohol were 1.2 (95% CI 0.9 to 1.6), 1.5 (1.1 to 2.1) and 1.4 (1.0 to 2.0) against below 100 mg (trend P = 0.02). More than 200 mg a day was associated with about 60-70 g lower birth weight. Risk rose with intake with no threshold. The association appeared stronger with faster caffeine clearance (interaction P = 0.06).

    There is inadequate evidence in humans and in experimental animals for the carcinogenicity of drinking coffee.     Finding
    In two-sample and one-sample genetic analyses, genetically predicted coffee consumption showed no change in risk of sporadic miscarriage, stillbirth or pre-term birth, or effect on gestational age. Birthweight associations were positive and inconsistent in size (two-sample, six variants: 0.050 SD per cup a day, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.098). Assuming the genetic score explained 0.37% of variance in coffee intake, UK Biobank had 80% power to detect a 26.9 g difference in birthweight or 0.03 more stillbirths per daily cup. In ALSPAC, self-reported coffee at week 32 was associated with -9.63 g birthweight per cup (-17.57 to -1.69), attenuated to -4.52 g (-14.92 to 5.88) after adjustment for smoking and alcohol. The authors note that a null association may reflect a lack of power.

    Finding
    In APOE3Leiden mice, cafestol raised serum cholesterol by 40% and triglycerides by 62%. Cafestol activated the ligand-binding domains and full-length forms of FXR and PXR in cell assays and recruited the coactivator SRC-1. In wild-type but not FXR-null mice, cafestol feeding repressed hepatic CYP7A1, CYP8B1 and NTCP, without inducing hepatic SHP or other positive FXR targets; it induced intestinal IBABP and FGF15, the latter through both FXR and PXR, and PXR-dependent intestinal CYP3A11, ABCA1 and CYP27A1. Cafestol does not raise serum cholesterol in wild-type mice; the relevance of the FXR-SHP repression of CYP7A1 to humans remains to be established.
