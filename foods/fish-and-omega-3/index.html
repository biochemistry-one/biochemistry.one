Foods

Fish and omega-3 fats

In brief

Oily fish supply two long omega-3 fats, EPA and DHA, which the body makes only in small amounts from a shorter omega-3 fat found in some plant oils. Fish also contain methylmercury, which can harm the developing brain. In large trials, fish-oil capsules did not prevent heart attacks and strokes taken together; a trial of a purified EPA drug that found a benefit compared it with a mineral-oil placebo that may have raised risk.

What it is

Oily fish supply two long omega-3 fats, EPA and DHA; some plant oils supply a shorter one, α-linolenic acid (ALA) 12.

Per 100 g, raw farmed Atlantic salmon contains 0.862 g eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5 n-3) and 1.1 g docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6 n-3), and cold-pressed linseed oil 53.4 g ALA (18:3 n-3) 1. Fish are the main dietary source of methylmercury; predatory species contain the most mercury 3.

In the body

The body converts only small amounts of ALA to EPA and DHA 4.

Humans lack Δ12- and Δ15-desaturases, so ALA is essential 4. FADS2 catalyses the first, rate-limiting Δ6-desaturation; ELOVL5 and FADS1 complete EPA, and ELOVL2, FADS2 and one peroxisomal β-oxidation cycle convert EPA to DHA 5.

Estimated conversion is 8–12% to EPA and possibly under 1% to DHA; dietary linoleic acid, converted by the same enzymes, lowers it 4. Homozygotes for the derived FADS haplotype D had 24% more plasma DHA than those for haplotype A 6.

Methylmercury crosses the placenta and blood–brain barrier; EFSA derived its tolerable weekly intake, 1.3 µg/kg body weight, from prenatal neurodevelopment, and high fish consumers may exceed it up to about sixfold 3.

What studies show

In independently funded research, fish-oil capsules did not reduce heart attacks and strokes taken together 78.

VITAL gave 25,871 US adults without cardiovascular disease 1 g fish oil daily (460 mg EPA, 380 mg DHA) or placebo; over a median 5.3 years, major cardiovascular events occurred in 3.0% against 3.2% (hazard ratio 0.92, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.06) 7.

In a meta-analysis of 10 trials in 77,917 high-risk people (mean 4.4 years), the rate ratio for major vascular events was 0.97 (0.93 to 1.01) 8.

Industry-funded results

ASCEND, whose capsule suppliers funded packaging and commented on its design and manuscript, gave 15,480 UK adults with diabetes the same dose or olive oil 9. Over a mean 7.4 years, serious vascular events occurred in 8.9% against 9.2% (rate ratio 0.97, 0.87 to 1.08) 9.

REDUCE-IT, sponsored and analysed by Amarin, gave 8179 statin-treated patients with raised triglycerides 4 g daily of icosapent ethyl (EPA ethyl ester) or a mineral-oil placebo 10. Over a median 4.9 years, the primary composite occurred in 17.2% against 22.0% (hazard ratio 0.75, 0.68 to 0.83), a difference of 4.8 percentage points (number needed to treat 21), and hospitalisation for atrial fibrillation or flutter in 3.1% against 2.1% 10.

STRENGTH, funded by AstraZeneca, compared 4 g daily of EPA and DHA carboxylic acids with corn oil in 13,078 statin-treated patients; it was stopped for futility at a median 42.0 months 11. Primary events occurred in 12.0% against 12.2% (hazard ratio 0.99, 0.90 to 1.09), and new atrial fibrillation in 2.2% against 1.3% (number needed to harm 114) 11.

Consensus statements

EFSA links 1–2 seafood servings a week, up to 3–4 in pregnancy, with better child neurodevelopment and lower adult coronary mortality 2. NICE advises against omega-3 compounds to prevent cardiovascular disease, except icosapent ethyl with a statin in established cardiovascular disease, fasting triglycerides ≥1.7 mmol/l and LDL cholesterol >1.04 and ≤2.60 mmol/l 1213.

Unresolved questions

NICE judged the REDUCE-IT effect uncertain because the mineral-oil placebo may have caused harm 13. LDL cholesterol rose 10.2% with mineral oil and 3.1% with icosapent ethyl, a difference the authors judge unlikely to explain the 25% lower risk 10. STRENGTH’s authors cite a rise of over 30% in C-reactive protein with mineral oil, unlike corn oil 11.

    EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA). Scientific Opinion on health benefits of seafood (fish and shellfish) consumption in relation to health risks associated with exposure to methylmercury EFSA Journal, 2014.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2014.3761

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Question No EFSA-Q-2013-00167).
    Finding
    Seafood is a source of protein, iodine, selenium, calcium and vitamins A and D, and provides n-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids. Consumption of about 1-2 servings of seafood per week and up to 3-4 servings per week during pregnancy has been associated with better functional outcomes of neurodevelopment in children compared to no seafood; such amounts have also been associated with a lower risk of coronary heart disease mortality in adults. These associations refer to seafood per se, including adverse effects of contaminants such as methylmercury; no additional benefit on neurodevelopment and no benefit on coronary mortality might be expected at higher intakes.

  3. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM). Scientific Opinion on the risk for public health related to the presence of mercury and methylmercury in food EFSA Journal, 2012.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2012.2985

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Question No EFSA-Q-2011-00923).
    Finding
    Methylmercury is by far the most common form of organic mercury in the food chain; mercury content varied widely among fish species and was highest in predatory fish; tuna, swordfish, cod, whiting and pike were major contributors to adult exposure. Methylmercury is much more extensively and rapidly absorbed than mercuric and mercurous mercury, more than 90% of blood methylmercury is in erythrocytes, and it crosses the placenta and the blood-brain barrier; absorbed methylmercury is excreted mainly in faeces as mercuric mercury. On the basis of prenatal exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes, the Panel established a tolerable weekly intake for methylmercury of 1.3 µg/kg body weight, expressed as mercury, against the JECFA provisional tolerable weekly intake of 1.6 µg/kg. The 95th percentile dietary exposure is close to or above the TWI for all age groups; high fish consumers, which might include pregnant women, may exceed it by up to approximately six-fold; unborn children are the most vulnerable group. The opinion does not assess the nutritional benefits of fish.

  4. Regulator

    EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition, and Allergies (NDA). Scientific Opinion on Dietary Reference Values for fats, including saturated fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, trans fatty acids, and cholesterol EFSA Journal, 2010.

    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Question No EFSA-Q-2008-466).
    Finding
    Humans lack delta-12 and delta-15 desaturases, so linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid are essential; n-6 and n-3 fatty acids are desaturated and elongated by the same enzymes. Adequate intake 4% of energy for linoleic acid and 0.5% for alpha-linolenic acid. Less than 1% of deuterated linoleic acid was converted to arachidonic acid; alpha-linolenic acid conversion about 8 to 12% to EPA and less than 1% to DHA; conversion of ALA decreases as dietary linoleic acid increases; amounts of n-3 LCPUFA formed depend on ALA intake, not on the n-3/n-6 ratio. Linoleic acid has a negative, dose-dependent relationship with LDL cholesterol. No specific values set for the n-3/n-6 ratio because data on clinical and biochemical endpoints in humans are insufficient to recommend a ratio independent of absolute intakes. No consistent evidence that n-6 PUFA intake has detrimental effects; no upper level set.

    Two common FADS haplotypes, A and D, defined by 28 SNPs across 38.9 kb.     Funding and finding
    Funding
    not stated
    Finding
    Icosapent ethyl is recommended as an option for reducing the risk of cardiovascular events in adults with a high risk of cardiovascular events, raised fasting triglycerides (1.7 mmol/litre or above) and statin treatment, only if they have established cardiovascular disease and LDL cholesterol above 1.04 mmol/litre and below or equal to 2.60 mmol/litre; it is unlikely to be cost effective for primary prevention and is not recommended for it.
