Foods

Red and processed meat

In brief

Red meat is the muscle of mammals such as cattle, pigs and sheep, and processed meat has been cured, salted or smoked. Substances in meat, or formed when it is cured or cooked hot, can damage DNA in the bowel, and gut bacteria convert its carnitine into a compound studied in heart disease. People who eat more of it have slightly higher rates of bowel cancer, heart disease and death, and expert panels judge this evidence differently.

What it is

Red meat is unprocessed mammalian muscle, such as beef, pork or lamb; processed meat has been salted, cured, fermented or smoked 1.

Potassium or sodium nitrite (E 249, E 250) is added in curing to fix colour and inhibit microbial growth 2.

In the body

Substances in meat, or formed when it is cured or cooked hot, can damage DNA 1.

Haem catalyses nitrosation and lipid peroxidation in the gut 1. In 21 volunteers given 420 g of red meat daily for 15 to 21 days, against a vegetarian diet, faecal N-nitroso compounds rose from 193 to 667 μg/day and O6-carboxymethylguanine-positive colonocytes from 22.7% to 55.7% 3.

Meat and meat products are the main dietary source of carcinogenic N-nitrosamines; EFSA judges 95th-percentile intake a health concern 4.

Heterocyclic aromatic amines such as PhIP form in meat cooked at high temperature 5. Hepatic CYP1A2 N-hydroxylates PhIP; NAT2 or SULT1A1 esterifies the product, which forms the DNA adduct dG-C8-PhIP 56.

Gut bacteria cleave carnitine, abundant in red meat, to trimethylamine, which hepatic FMO3 oxidises to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) 786. In 113 adults, plasma TMAO rose about threefold after 4 weeks of a red-meat diet against white-meat or meat-free diets 8.

What studies show

People who eat more red and processed meat have slightly higher rates of heart disease, bowel cancer and death 910.

In six US cohorts (29,682 adults, median 19 years), each 2 extra weekly servings (2 against 0 for processed meat and cardiovascular disease) were associated with 30-year absolute risk increases in cardiovascular disease and death of 0.62 and 0.76 percentage points for unprocessed red meat (hazard ratios 1.03 each) and 1.74 and 0.90 for processed meat (1.07 and 1.03) 9.

In UK Biobank (mean 5.7 years), colorectal cancer occurred in 274 of 68,359 people (0.40%) eating red and processed meat less than twice a week and 1209 of 192,600 (0.63%) eating it four or more times (adjusted hazard ratio 1.20, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.37), an association confined to men 10.

Industry-funded results

In a meta-analysis of 27 cohorts, partly funded by Meat & Livestock Australia, high against low red meat intake gave a relative risk of colorectal cancer of 1.11 (1.03 to 1.19); the authors concluded that red meat is not an independent predictor 11.

Consensus statements

In 2015 the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), by majority on sufficient evidence for colorectal cancer, and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A), on limited human and strong mechanistic evidence 12. A cited meta-analysis of ten cohorts gave relative risks of 1.17 (1.05 to 1.31) per 100 g/day of red meat and 1.18 (1.10 to 1.28) per 50 g/day of processed meat 12.

In 2019 the NutriRECS panel suggested continuing current consumption of both (weak recommendations, low-certainty evidence) 13. For 3 fewer weekly servings of unprocessed red meat it estimated 1 to 6 fewer cardiometabolic events per 1000 over 10.8 years and 7 fewer lifetime cancer deaths per 1000 13.

Unresolved questions

IARC judged chance, bias and confounding unlikely explanations for processed meat; NutriRECS declined to upgrade the cohort evidence for dose–response, citing possible residual confounding 1213.

The guideline declared no primary funding source 13. A Texas A&M AgriLife Research grant and earlier funding from the International Life Sciences Institute (ILSI) North America were later added to the lead author’s disclosures 14. An industry-supported ILSI committee funded his 2017 review of sugar guidelines 15.

Whether TMAO contributes to cardiovascular disease in humans is unclear 8.

    Turesky RJ. Mechanistic Evidence for Red Meat and Processed Meat Intake and Cancer Risk: A Follow-up on the International Agency for Research on Cancer Evaluation of 2015 Chimia, 2018.

    doi:10.2533/chimia.2018.718PubMed 30376922

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    The research conducted in the author's lab on cooked meat mutagens and colorectal cancer has been supported by National Institutes of Health Grants R01CA122320 and R01CA134700.
    Finding
    Narrative review. Red meat: unprocessed mammalian muscle meats, including beef, veal, pork, lamb, mutton, horse or goat. Processed meat: meat treated through salting, curing, fermentation, smoking or other processes to enhance flavour or improve preservation. Curing with nitrite can produce carcinogenic N-nitroso compounds, at less than 1 to 130 ppb in cured meats, activated by CYP2E1. Ingested haem can catalyse the nitrosation of endogenous secondary amines and exert pro-oxidative effects by catalysis of lipid peroxidation in the gastrointestinal tract. High-temperature cooking produces heterocyclic aromatic amines, which cytochrome P450 enzymes N-hydroxylate and N-acetyltransferases or sulfotransferases convert to DNA-binding intermediates. Many of these chemicals form DNA adducts.

    EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS), Mortensen A, Aguilar F, Crebelli R, Di Domenico A, Dusemund B, et al. Re-evaluation of potassium nitrite (E 249) and sodium nitrite (E 250) as food additives EFSA Journal, 2017.

    Finding
    Sodium and potassium nitrite are used in curing mixtures to develop and fix the colour of meat, to inhibit microbial growth and to develop characteristic flavours. Acceptable daily intake 0.07 mg nitrite ion/kg body weight per day, based on methaemoglobin formation. Use as food additives supplied about 17% (range 1.5 to 36.0%) of overall nitrite exposure. Margin of exposure for endogenous NDMA formed from nitrite at the ADI 4.2 × 10^5; for preformed NDMA plus NDEA in processed food, below 10,000 in all age groups at high exposure. Nitrosamines formed from nitrite added at authorised levels could not be clearly distinguished from those present without added nitrite. There was evidence linking preformed NDMA with colorectal cancer and some evidence linking nitrite plus nitrate from processed meat with colorectal cancer.

  5. Independent research

    Bellamri M, Walmsley SJ, Turesky RJ. Metabolism and biomarkers of heterocyclic aromatic amines in humans Genes and Environment, 2021.

    doi:10.1186/s41021-021-00200-7PubMed 34271992

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    R01CA122320 and the National Cancer Institute Cancer Center Support Grant CA077598 supported a portion of the research conducted in the Turesky Laboratory.
    Finding
    Narrative review. Heterocyclic aromatic amines form during high-temperature cooking of meat, poultry and fish, in amounts that increase with cooking time and temperature; PhIP and AαC can reach 50 ppb or more in well-done meat and poultry. In model systems with amino acids and sugars, creatine or creatinine is the precursor of the imidazo moieties of the aminoimidazoarenes, which are more potent mutagens than the amino acid pyrolysates. Hepatic CYP1A2 accounts for an estimated 70% of PhIP metabolism in humans, mainly by N-oxidation to genotoxic N-hydroxy-PhIP. Most N-hydroxy-heterocyclic amines are bioactivated by human NAT2 to reactive esters; N-acetoxy-PhIP is stable enough to isolate; SULT1A1 and SULT1A2 catalyse binding of N-hydroxy-PhIP to DNA. The proposed reactive intermediate is the heteroaryl nitrenium ion; N-hydroxy-PhIP forms the adduct N-(2′-deoxyguanosin-8-yl)-PhIP (dG-C8-PhIP).

    Finding
    A two-component Rieske-type oxygenase/reductase, CntA and CntB, encoded in a carnitine utilisation gene cluster in 39 human microbiota reference genomes, cleaves the C-N bond of carnitine to trimethylamine and a four-carbon compound.     Finding

  12. Expert consensus

    Bouvard V, Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, El Ghissassi F, Benbrahim-Tallaa L, et al. Carcinogenicity of consumption of red and processed meat Lancet Oncology, 2015.

    doi:10.1016/S1470-2045(15)00444-1PubMed 26514947

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    not stated
    Finding
    Red meat: unprocessed mammalian muscle meat such as beef, veal, pork, lamb, mutton, horse or goat. Processed meat: meat transformed through salting, curing, fermentation, smoking or other processes to enhance flavour or improve preservation. More than 800 epidemiological studies assessed. Colorectal cancer: positive associations with red meat in 7 of 14 cohorts and with processed meat in 12 of 18 cohorts; a meta-analysis of 10 cohorts gave relative risks of 1.17 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.31) per 100 g/day of red meat and 1.18 (1.10 to 1.28) per 50 g/day of processed meat. Processed meat classified as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) on sufficient evidence for colorectal cancer; chance, bias and confounding judged unlikely explanations. Red meat classified as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A) on limited evidence in humans and strong mechanistic evidence; chance, bias and confounding could not be ruled out with the same confidence. Mechanistic evidence: haem iron mediates formation of N-nitroso compounds and lipid oxidation products in the digestive tract; 300 or 420 g/day of red meat increased putative N-nitroso-derived DNA adducts in two intervention studies; meat heated at high temperature contains heterocyclic aromatic amines, which are genotoxic.

    Finding
    The panel suggests that adults continue current unprocessed red meat consumption and continue current processed meat consumption (weak recommendations, low-certainty evidence); 11 of 14 panellists voted for each, 3 for a weak recommendation to reduce. The panel did not rate up for dose-response because of potential residual confounding. Randomised trials were limited by small differences in meat consumption between groups.
