Foods

Dairy

In brief

Most adults lose the enzyme that digests lactose, the sugar in milk, but a common genetic variant keeps it active in most people of northern European descent. Most studies that follow large groups of people find no link between milk intake and fractures, and a care-home trial that found fewer fractures with extra dairy foods was funded by dairy organisations.

What it is

Milk is mostly water 1.

Per 100 g, whole milk contains 88.1 g water, 4.81 g of the sugar lactose, 3.27 g protein, 3.20 g fat and 123 mg calcium 1.

What happens in the body

An enzyme in the small intestine, lactase, splits lactose into two sugars, galactose and glucose 2.

Lactase is encoded by LCT 2. In most humans lactase declines after weaning 3. It persists in over 90% of Swedes and Danes and in about 1% of Chinese 3. In Europeans, persistence is mediated by LCT −13910 C>T (rs4988235) 45.

Undigested lactose reaches the colon, where bacteria ferment it to hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and short-chain fatty acids 4.

What studies show

In studies that follow large groups of people, no link between milk and fractures was found in 25 of 28 analyses 6.

In a Swedish cohort, 61,433 women were followed for a mean 20.1 years 7. Three or more glasses of milk a day, against less than one, was associated with 4.2 against 3.1 hip fractures per 1000 person-years (age standardised; adjusted hazard ratio 1.60, 95% CI 1.39 to 1.84) 7. Deaths were 17.7 against 10.7 per 1000 person-years (1.93, 1.80 to 2.06) 7.

In three US cohorts, 217,755 adults were followed for up to 32 years 8. The highest against the lowest dairy intake was associated with a hazard ratio for death of 1.07 (1.04 to 1.10) 8.

In the same US cohorts, total dairy was not associated with type 2 diabetes 9. Each daily serving of yoghurt was associated with a hazard ratio of 0.83 (0.75 to 0.92) 9.

The lactase-persistence T allele of rs4988235, a genetic instrument for lifelong milk intake, was associated with lower odds of coronary artery disease (odds ratio 0.86, 0.75 to 0.99; 22,233 cases) 10.

In a meta-analysis of 32 cohort studies, prostate cancer risk was higher per 400 g of dairy a day (relative risk 1.07, 1.02 to 1.12) 11.

Industry-funded results

A cluster-randomised trial included 7195 residents of 60 Australian aged-care homes 12. Over a mean 12.6 months, extra milk, yoghurt and cheese reduced fractures from 203 (5.2%) to 121 (3.7%) (hazard ratio 0.67, 0.48 to 0.93) 12. In a post hoc analysis, the foods reduced hip fractures from 93 (2.4%) to 42 (1.3%) (0.54, 0.35 to 0.83) 12. The numbers needed to treat were 52 and 82 12. Funders included Dairy Australia, Fonterra and the Dutch Dairy Association, and Fonterra donated the foods 12. The paper states that the funders had no role in the study 12.

Consensus statements

A National Institutes of Health consensus panel concluded in 2010 that adults and adolescents with diagnosed lactose malabsorption could take at least 12 g of lactose in one dose with no or minor symptoms 13. That amount is the lactose in a cup of milk 13.

Unresolved questions

Whether milk itself alters fracture risk or mortality is unsettled 67.

Processing

Cheese-making, fermentation and lactase treatment lower the lactose content of dairy foods 4.

Cheddar contains 0.16 g lactose per 100 g 1. Yoghurt contains about half the lactose of milk 4. Live cultures in yoghurt improve digestion of its lactose in people with lactose maldigestion 14. Of 14 human studies, 13 showed the effect 14.

    Finding
    In most humans lactase-phlorizin hydrolase, which hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose in the small intestine, declines after weaning. Lactase persistence is inherited as a dominant trait in Europeans and its frequency exceeds 90% in Swedes and Danes, is about 50% in Spanish and French populations and about 1% in Chinese. C/T-13910 lies about 14 kb upstream of LCT within MCM6. Three African variants, G/C-14010, T/G-13915 and C/G-13907, arose on different haplotypes, are associated with lactase persistence and raise LCT promoter activity in vitro by about 18-30%; selection coefficients 0.04-0.097.

    Misselwitz B, Butter M, Verbeke K, Fox MR. Update on lactose malabsorption and intolerance: pathogenesis, diagnosis and clinical management Gut, 2019.

    Funding and finding
    Funding

    Finding
    Narrative review, commissioned. In lactose malabsorption, colonic bacteria ferment lactose to hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and short-chain fatty acids; the osmotic load in the colon rises about eightfold. Lactase persistence in Europeans is mediated by LCT-13910 C>T within intron 13 of MCM6, 13.9 kb upstream of LCT. Cow's milk contains about 5 g lactose per 100 mL; yoghurt contains about 50% of the lactose of unprocessed milk, and cheese has low lactose content, especially long-ripened cheese. Lactose-free dairy products are made by adding lactase to milk.

    Suchy FJ, Brannon PM, Carpenter TO, Fernandez JR, Gilsanz V, Gould JB, et al. National Institutes of Health Consensus Development Conference: lactose intolerance and health Annals of Internal Medicine, 2010.

    doi:10.7326/0003-4819-152-12-201006150-00248PubMed 20404261

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    Conference sponsored by the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development and the NIH Office of Medical Applications of Research; cosponsors NIDDK, NIH Division of Nutrition Research Coordination, National Institute on Aging and Office of Dietary Supplements; partners US Department of Agriculture and Health Resources and Services Administration.     Finding
    Adults and adolescents with diagnosed lactose malabsorption could ingest at least 12 g of lactose in a single dose (the lactose in 1 cup of milk) with no or minor symptoms; 50 g usually induced symptoms when taken without meals. Lactose does not necessarily affect the efficiency of calcium absorption, and persons with lactase non-persistence do not have substantial impairment in calcium absorption.

    EFSA Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA). Scientific Opinion on the substantiation of health claims related to live yoghurt cultures and improved lactose digestion (ID 1143, 2976) pursuant to Article 13(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 EFSA Journal, 2010.

    doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1763

    Funding and finding
    Funding
    European Food Safety Authority, on request from the European Commission (Questions EFSA-Q-2008-1882, EFSA-Q-2008-3708). The evidence assessed was supplied by Member States and stakeholders.
    Finding
    Thirteen of 14 human studies showed enhanced digestion of lactose in yoghurt in lactose maldigesters when live Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus were ingested in yoghurt; the panel concluded that a cause and effect relationship is established, with at least 10^8 CFU live starter microorganisms per gram. In one study about one fifth of the lactase activity contained in yoghurt reached the terminal ileum; in another the effect was destroyed by pasteurisation.
